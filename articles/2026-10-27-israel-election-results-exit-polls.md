> *Pray for the peace of Jerusalem: "May they be secure who love you!"* (Psalm 122:6)

Israelis voted on Tuesday 27 October 2026 in their first general election since the Hamas attack of 7 October 2023, and in record numbers. The exit polls on Tuesday night said nobody had won, and the real count on Wednesday has said the same thing with harder numbers. Benjamin Netanyahu's Likud is the largest party and his bloc leads, but it is seven seats short of a majority. The bloc led by former army chief Gadi Eisenkot is further back. The two Arab lists, with a record 18 seats between them, hold the balance that neither side wants to depend on.

This article gives the count as it stands, the exit polls it replaced, what happens next, and how Christians might pray. We keep updating it; the final tally is expected by Friday and the official result on 4 November.

**Update, Wednesday 28 October, 11 am Israel time.** With about two thirds of the vote counted, the Central Elections Committee's running tally puts Netanyahu's coalition bloc on 54 seats, Eisenkot's change bloc on 48, the Joint List on 12 and Ra'am on 6. Likud has 27 seats to Yashar's 21. The coalition bloc had stood at 56 and then 57 in the early hours and has slipped as the cities and Arab towns were counted; Ra'am, which sat at 3.43 per cent and four seats at nine in the morning, has climbed to six. Two lists, the Reservists-Economic party of Yoaz Hendel and Yaron Zelekha and Ofer Winter's Amcha Yisrael, are below the 3.25 per cent threshold. The committee stresses that the figures are partial and will move again when the soldiers' and absentee ballots are counted later in the week. The details are below.

The blocs on the count: Netanyahu's coalition bloc (Likud, Shas, United Torah Judaism, Otzma Yehudit, Religious Zionism) 54; Eisenkot's change bloc (Yashar, Together, the Democrats, Yisrael Beytenu) 48; the Arab lists (Joint List and Ra'am) 18. Later in the morning, with 72 per cent counted, the Times of Israel reported the same 54, 48 and 18.

Two things stand out. The exit polls underestimated Likud by two or three seats and, as the networks themselves admitted overnight, overstated the change bloc. And the Arab parties have done better than any poll predicted: 18 seats between them is the most either list has ever held, on a turnout in Arab towns that reached about 80 per cent.

The blocs, as the networks drew them:

Several small lists, including the Reservists party of Ofer Winter and Yaron Zelekha's Economic party, fell below the 3.25 per cent threshold on every poll, so their votes count for nothing and the final seat shares will shift once the real ballots are in. Exit polls in Israel have been wrong by several seats before.

## The revised polls and the first count

Exit polls in Israel are taken until a couple of hours before the polls close, and the networks revise them as late samples come in. On Tuesday night the revisions ran one way. Channel 12 went from 50 for Netanyahu's bloc and 52 for Eisenkot's to 52 and 49, with the Joint List and Ra'am together on 19. Channel 13 moved the Arab parties up to 18 and Eisenkot's bloc down to 51. i24NEWS's own poll had Likud 27, Yashar 20, Together 15, Shas 8, the Democrats, Joint List and Ra'am on 9, 9 and 7, United Torah Judaism and Otzma Yehudit 7 each, Religious Zionism 6 and Yisrael Beytenu 5, for blocs of 55 to 49 with the Arab parties on 16. Channel 14's late update gave Netanyahu 61, a majority of one, but only by assuming Avigdor Liberman's party misses the 3.25 per cent threshold and its votes are redistributed.

Then the real count began. At 2.30 am the Central Elections Committee said it had counted its first 100,000 ballots: 27 per cent for Likud, 17 per cent for Yashar, 9 per cent for Together. By 37 per cent counted the coalition bloc was on 57; at 55 per cent it was 56 to 49; at 66 per cent, 54 to 48, where it has stayed into the late morning. The hundreds of thousands of soldiers' and absentee ballots, which have moved seats in past elections, come last and are not expected to be fully counted before Thursday.

## A record turnout

By eight in the evening turnout stood at 74.3 per cent, against 66.3 per cent at the same hour last time, and at the close it was 77.7 per cent, the highest since 1999 and within a point of the all-time record of 1973. Turnout in Arab towns was among the highest in the country. Among Israelis voting at embassies abroad it was 86 per cent. Whatever else the night showed, Israelis wanted to be heard.

## What the two leaders said

Gadi Eisenkot, the former chief of staff who entered politics after losing a son in Gaza, wrote on X: "If these are the final results, this is a victory for Israel's responsible Zionist majority," and promised a government "committed to universal conscription as soon as possible." Naftali Bennett told his supporters after midnight: "The alliance of those who serve has defeated the alliance of draft dodgers. Netanyahu has lost, and the October 7 government is going home." Yair Lapid said "the State of Israel saved itself today" and that the opposition, as the side with more votes, had the duty to form a government, "not perfect on day one" and perhaps not broad, but one it would expand. Those claims rest on the first wave of polls; the revised ones are less kind to them.

Eisenkot told his party's headquarters later: "This will be a new start. A real new start on a new path. The old era is over, and Israel is starting a new era." Yair Golan of the Democrats wrote that voters had "fired the Netanyahu government."

Netanyahu, speaking after the revised polls and the first count, claimed the opposite. "What we've seen is that the Likud party is the largest party. Nobody can compete with us," he said. "The second thing that is clear as well is that the right bloc has a majority. And it is a decisive majority." He called for a "national right-wing government" and accused the television channels of projecting stronger opposition results to discourage his voters. Before the polls closed he had posted a video calling the high turnout in Arab towns part of an effort to "steal" the election, and he has said that working with the Arab parties' leaders would be "a disgrace and a shame." Gideon Sa'ar mocked the "smugness" of commentators who had written the coalition off. Itamar Ben-Gvir, whose Otzma Yehudit holds seven seats on the count, said: "We are very proud of our journey." Bezalel Smotrich promised to continue settlement expansion.

The count does not bear out Netanyahu's "decisive majority": his bloc is seven seats short, and the right's hope now rests on the soldiers' ballots, which usually lean right, lifting it toward 61 or pulling a small party below the threshold.

The man both sides were calling was Mansour Abbas of Ra'am. "Everyone is speaking with us, not just from the change bloc," he told Channel 12, adding that "with the numbers we have now, there is no government." By Wednesday, with his party up to six seats, he was saying he hoped the final count would let the change bloc and Ra'am reach 61 together. Yair Golan of the Democrats said he would not sit with Likud under Netanyahu or with the haredi parties, but did not rule out a coalition with the Arab parties.

## What happens next

The Central Elections Committee counts through Wednesday and Thursday; soldiers', diplomats' and prisoners' ballots, the "double envelopes," come last, are not expected to be fully counted before Thursday, and have swung seats before. The final tally is expected by Friday and the official results are published on 4 November. President Isaac Herzog then consults every party that won seats and asks the member of Knesset most likely to assemble 61 to try, with 28 days to do it and a possible 14-day extension before the task passes to someone else, or the country votes again.

The arithmetic is unforgiving. Netanyahu's bloc of Likud, Shas, United Torah Judaism, Religious Zionism and Otzma Yehudit is at 54 on the count. Eisenkot's bloc of Yashar, Together, the Democrats and Yisrael Beytenu is at 48, and even with Ra'am's six it reaches only 54; its leaders have ruled out governing with the Joint List, though Mansour Abbas has said he is willing to repeat his 2021 partnership, and the Joint List has offered a "safety net" from outside a coalition in return for commitments on crime and Palestinian statehood. The other roads are a unity government, a rotating premiership, a different prime minister from one of the camps, or a sixth election in seven years, during which Netanyahu would stay on as caretaker.

## Why the result was so close

This was the first election since 7 October 2023 and the two-year war that followed in Gaza, Lebanon, Iran and beyond. Netanyahu's government was the first to serve a full term since 1973, but it did so through mass protests over judicial reform, an International Criminal Court warrant for its prime minister, and the collapse of its relationship with the ultra-Orthodox parties, which left the coalition in 2025 over military conscription for yeshiva students. Eisenkot, who sat in the war cabinet after 7 October, built his campaign on competence and accountability for that day. The voters split almost exactly down the middle.

## Questions this article answers

### Who won the Israel election on 27 October 2026?

Nobody yet. With about two thirds of the vote counted on Wednesday 28 October, Benjamin Netanyahu's bloc has 54 seats and Gadi Eisenkot's 48, both short of the 61 needed; the Arab parties hold 18. Likud is the largest party with 27 seats. The soldiers' ballots are counted later in the week, the final tally is expected by Friday and the official result on 4 November.

### How many seats did Likud and Yashar win?

On the count so far Likud has 27 seats and Eisenkot's Yashar 21, with Together, led by Naftali Bennett and Yair Lapid, on 11. The exit polls had put Likud at 24 to 30 and Yashar at 19 to 23.

### How many seats did the Arab parties win?

A record 18 on the count: 12 for the Joint List and 6 for Ra'am, more than either list has ever held. Neither main bloc can reach 61 without them or without a unity deal, and the opposition's leaders have ruled out governing with the Joint List.

### What happens after the Israeli election?

The Central Elections Committee finishes the count by about Friday and confirms the result on 4 November. President Isaac Herzog then consults the parties and asks the candidate most likely to form a coalition to try, with 28 days and a possible 14-day extension. If nobody succeeds, Israel could face another election.

### Why was this Israeli election important?

It was the first since 7 October 2023 and the war that followed, and a verdict on Netanyahu's government, which served a full term through protests over judicial reform, an International Criminal Court warrant and a split with the ultra-Orthodox parties over conscription. Turnout was the highest in decades.
