> *Pray for the peace of Jerusalem: "May they be secure who love you!"* (Psalm 122:6)

Israelis voted on Tuesday 27 October 2026 in their first general election since the Hamas attack of 7 October 2023, and in record numbers. The exit polls on Tuesday night said nobody had won, and the final count, published by the Central Elections Committee in the small hours of Friday, has said the same thing with hard numbers. Benjamin Netanyahu's Likud is the largest party with 27 seats and his bloc leads with 53, eight short of a majority. The bloc led by former army chief Gadi Eisenkot has 48. The two Arab lists, with a record 19 seats between them, hold the balance that neither side wanted to depend on, and the question of the week is whether Eisenkot will take their hand. President Isaac Herzog begins his consultations with the parties on Wednesday.

This article gives the final result, the counts and exit polls it replaced, where the coalition talks stand, and how Christians might pray. We keep updating it as the talks move.

**Update, Sunday 1 November.** The final result is in. After the "double envelopes" of soldiers, diplomats, prisoners and hospital patients were added, Likud rose a seat to 27 and Ra'am a seat to 8, while Shas fell to 6, its worst result since 1992, and the Joint List to 11. The blocs did not move: Netanyahu's coalition 53, Eisenkot's change bloc 48, the Arab parties 19. Turnout was 77.9 per cent, with 5,716,665 votes cast, and the threshold of 3.25 per cent came to 184,947 votes, which no other list reached. The committee hands the result to President Herzog on Wednesday 4 November, the day he starts hearing the parties' recommendations, and the new Knesset is to be sworn in on 10 November. Over the weekend the lines hardened. Eisenkot opened the door a crack to the Arab parties with four conditions for anyone who wants to join him; Netanyahu called that "a disaster for Israel"; Avigdor Liberman stopped answering his partners' calls and said he would sit "neither with Bibi nor with Tibi"; the police said there was no evidence for Likud's claim that the election was "stolen"; and thousands rallied in Tel Aviv on Saturday night for a Jewish-Arab government. The details are below.

## The final result, party by party

These are the Central Elections Committee's final results, published early on Friday 30 October after the double-envelope count, set beside the partial count of the regular polling stations from Wednesday night and the two main exit polls from election night. Seats are out of 120; a government needs 61.

The blocs: Netanyahu's coalition bloc (Likud, Shas, United Torah Judaism, Otzma Yehudit, Religious Zionism) 53; Eisenkot's change bloc (Yashar, Together, the Democrats, Yisrael Beytenu) 48; the Arab lists (Joint List and Ra'am) 19. The soldiers' and absentee ballots, which have swung seats in past elections, moved only two this time, and both within a bloc: one from Shas to Likud and one from the Joint List to Ra'am. The committee says the figures are still subject to its integrity checks and "minor adjustments," and they become official when they are handed to the president on 4 November.

Three things stand out. The exit polls had Likud about right in the end but, as the networks themselves admitted overnight, overstated the change bloc. Shas, which went into the election with 11 seats, came out with 6, its lowest since 1992. And the Arab parties have done better than any poll predicted: nearly 840,000 people voted for the Joint List and Ra'am, 64 per cent more than in 2022, on a turnout in Arab towns of about 80 per cent, and 19 seats is the most they have ever held.

The blocs, as the networks drew them:

- **Channel 12:** Eisenkot's bloc 52, Netanyahu's bloc 50, Arab parties 18.
- **Channel 13:** opposition 53, coalition 50, Arab parties 17.
- **Channel 14:** right-wing bloc 60, left-wing bloc 43, Arab parties 17, which would leave Netanyahu one seat short.
- **i24NEWS** gave Likud 27 and Yashar 20.

Several small lists, including the Reservists party of Ofer Winter and Yaron Zelekha's Economic party, fell below the 3.25 per cent threshold on every poll and in the count, so their votes counted for nothing. Exit polls in Israel have been wrong by several seats before, and they were again.

## How the count moved

Exit polls in Israel are taken until a couple of hours before the polls close, and the networks revise them as late samples come in. On Tuesday night the revisions ran one way. Channel 12 went from 50 for Netanyahu's bloc and 52 for Eisenkot's to 52 and 49, with the Joint List and Ra'am together on 19. Channel 13 moved the Arab parties up to 18 and Eisenkot's bloc down to 51. Channel 14's late update gave Netanyahu 61, a majority of one, but only by assuming Avigdor Liberman's party missed the threshold.

Then the real count began. At 2.30 am the Central Elections Committee said it had counted its first 100,000 ballots: 27 per cent for Likud, 17 per cent for Yashar, 9 per cent for Together. By 37 per cent counted the coalition bloc was on 57; at 55 per cent it was 56 to 49; at 66 per cent, 54 to 48; at 77.6 per cent, 54 to 49; at 88.6 per cent, with the regular stations all but done on Wednesday night, 53 to 48, where it stayed. The double envelopes were counted on Thursday and the final result was published at about half past midnight on Friday.

## A record turnout

By eight in the evening turnout stood at 74.3 per cent, against 66.3 per cent at the same hour last time, and the final figure was 77.9 per cent, the highest since 1999 and within a point of the all-time record of 1973. Turnout in Arab towns was among the highest in the country. Among Israelis voting at embassies abroad it was 86 per cent. Whatever else the night showed, Israelis wanted to be heard.

## What the two leaders said on election night

Gadi Eisenkot, the former chief of staff who entered politics after losing a son in Gaza, wrote on X: "If these are the final results, this is a victory for Israel's responsible Zionist majority," and promised a government "committed to universal conscription as soon as possible." Naftali Bennett told his supporters after midnight: "The alliance of those who serve has defeated the alliance of draft dodgers. Netanyahu has lost, and the October 7 government is going home." Yair Lapid said "the State of Israel saved itself today" and that the opposition, as the side with more votes, had the duty to form a government. Eisenkot told his party's headquarters later: "This will be a new start. A real new start on a new path. The old era is over, and Israel is starting a new era."

Netanyahu claimed the opposite. "What we've seen is that the Likud party is the largest party. Nobody can compete with us," he said. "The second thing that is clear as well is that the right bloc has a majority. And it is a decisive majority." He called for a "national right-wing government" and accused the television channels of projecting stronger opposition results to discourage his voters. Before the polls closed he had posted a video calling the high turnout in Arab towns part of an effort to "steal" the election. The count did not bear out the "decisive majority": his bloc finished eight seats short.

## The "stolen election" claim, and what the police found

On Wednesday Likud went further than its leader. In a statement it said the Arab parties had "stolen" the election through fraud, bribery, intimidation and ballot manipulation, with foreign money behind them, and called the result "an attack on democracy aimed at enabling a foreign takeover of the Knesset." The claim rested on the arrest of three Ra'am activists suspected of paying a small number of voters in one Arab city.

The answer came quickly. A police source said there was "no investigative- or intelligence-based supporting evidence for the claims." The director of the Central Elections Committee rejected the talk of widespread fraud as baseless, and the committee said the election had been conducted lawfully and there was no basis for claims of systematic interference with the integrity of the vote. Its chairman, Justice Noam Sohlberg, did ask the Shin Bet to examine the separate suspicion that foreign governments, Qatar first among them, had funded parts of the Arab campaign. The Shin Bet described that inquiry as "limited," and a source familiar with it told Haaretz that any effect on the result was probably minimal. On Friday the Mossad agreed, at the Shin Bet's request, to help with the foreign side of the inquiry; Ynet reported that Turkey was also being looked at. Qatar rejected the allegations as a disinformation campaign.

Eisenkot accused Netanyahu of trying to delegitimise the election. Netanyahu, he said, was now a caretaker prime minister "without a mandate and without public confidence," and he warned that the inquiry must not be used to delay the publication of the results or the swearing-in of the Knesset, from which he would not allow "a single centimetre" of deviation. He promised that his government would set up a state commission of inquiry into 7 October and pass a conscription law, and said he would back military action before a new government is formed only against direct and immediate threats.

## The coalition talks: Eisenkot's four conditions

Eisenkot spent the campaign ruling out a government that depends on the Arab parties, and three of the four parties in his bloc pledged the same. On Saturday night he moved. In a statement he said the record turnout and the challenges facing Israel demanded "an extraordinary effort" to avoid another election and a long political crisis, and that any member of Knesset who accepts four principles can be a partner in forming the next government, whatever party or bloc they come from. The four: Israel as a Jewish, democratic, liberal and egalitarian state, under its Declaration of Independence; military, national or civilian service for every citizen; recognition of Hamas as a terrorist organisation; and the destruction of Hamas's military and governing power in Gaza.

The door is open a crack, not wide. Eisenkot still says he does not want a government dependent on the Arab lists, and the Joint List's leaders say they do not want to be in one. Yousef Jabareen said the Joint List would support Eisenkot in the Knesset without joining his government, and Ahmad Tibi said the list would back replacing the Knesset speaker but ruled out ministries. Both the Joint List and Ra'am are expected to recommend Eisenkot to the president, and Mansour Abbas has said he is willing to repeat his 2021 partnership. On those assumptions Ynet counts 60 recommendations for Eisenkot (his bloc without Liberman, plus the 19 Arab seats) against 46 for Netanyahu (his bloc without United Torah Judaism), with Liberman's 7 and United Torah Judaism's 7 undecided. Sixty is one short of a majority, and recommendations are not a coalition.

Netanyahu answered the same evening on X: "Cooperation with Mansour Abbas and Tibi is a disaster for Israel." Likud accused Eisenkot of intending to cooperate with "parties that support terrorism" and called his statement "hysterical." Netanyahu's allies have been floating the other way out, a unity government with Eisenkot and a rotating premiership, which Eisenkot has refused again and again; a week before the vote he said that if he found himself in a rotation with Netanyahu "you would see me resign an hour later."

## Liberman walks away from the phone

The man who could decide it is Avigdor Liberman, whose seven seats would give Eisenkot and the Arab parties 67 or Netanyahu's bloc 60. On Thursday he told his party's activists: "We will not sit with Bibi or with Tibi." Channel 12 then reported that he had largely stopped answering calls from Eisenkot and Bennett, with only their staff still in touch, and that his view is that if anyone from the change bloc goes into a unity government with Netanyahu it should be Eisenkot, not him. Liberman denied a report that he is angling for the Knesset speaker's chair. Eisenkot and Bennett, meeting at Bennett's home in Ra'anana, issued a joint statement promising to work "to establish a good government in Israel as quickly as possible." Lapid's verdict on the week was shorter: "A win is a win."

## The street

On Saturday night thousands filled Habima Square in Tel Aviv to urge Eisenkot and his partners to make the deal with the Arab parties. Protesters told AFP this was the "last chance for real change" and called Netanyahu "a great manipulator." Netanyahu's supporters held a counter-protest, chanting that Israel should not have a government "with terrorists," and right-wing activists planned a demonstration outside Eisenkot's home. The Tel Aviv stock market, which fell about 1.5 per cent on the morning after the vote, is waiting like everyone else.

## What happens next

The Central Elections Committee hands the official result to President Herzog on Wednesday 4 November. That day he begins consulting every party that won seats and asks the member of Knesset most likely to assemble 61 to try, with 28 days to do it and a possible 14-day extension before the task passes to someone else, or the country votes again. Herzog has said: "We must do everything possible, especially after yesterday, to avoid another round of elections. So I expect everyone who comes here to arrive with an open mind." The 26th Knesset is sworn in on 10 November. Netanyahu stays prime minister until a new government is sworn in.

The arithmetic is unforgiving. Netanyahu's bloc is at 53 and has nowhere to grow except Liberman, who has refused, or a split in the change bloc. Eisenkot's bloc is at 48; with Ra'am it reaches 56, with the Joint List as well 67, but Liberman has refused to sit with the Joint List and Bennett has said the same, which is why the "safety net" of Arab support from outside the coalition is being discussed. The other roads are a unity government with a rotation, which Eisenkot refuses; a different prime minister from one of the camps; or a sixth election in seven years.

## Why the result was so close

This was the first election since 7 October 2023 and the two-year war that followed in Gaza, Lebanon, Iran and beyond. Netanyahu's government was the first to serve a full term since 1973, but it did so through mass protests over judicial reform, an International Criminal Court warrant for its prime minister, and the collapse of its relationship with the ultra-Orthodox parties, which left the coalition in 2025 over military conscription for yeshiva students. Eisenkot, who sat in the war cabinet after 7 October, built his campaign on competence and accountability for that day. The voters split almost exactly down the middle, and then the Arab citizens of Israel, voting in numbers they never had before, made the split a deadlock.

## Questions this article answers

### Who won the Israel election on 27 October 2026?

Nobody. The final count gives Benjamin Netanyahu's bloc 53 seats and Gadi Eisenkot's 48, both short of the 61 needed; the Arab parties hold a record 19. Likud is the largest party with 27 seats. President Herzog begins consulting the parties on 4 November and will ask one leader to try to form a government.

### How many seats did Likud and Yashar win?

Likud won 27 seats and Eisenkot's Yashar 21, with Together, led by Naftali Bennett and Yair Lapid, on 12. The exit polls had put Likud at 24 to 30 and Yashar at 19 to 23. Shas fell to 6, its lowest since 1992.

### How many seats did the Arab parties win?

A record 19: 11 for the Joint List and 8 for Ra'am, on nearly 840,000 votes, 64 per cent more than in 2022. Neither main bloc can reach 61 without them or without a unity deal. Both are expected to recommend Eisenkot to the president, and the Joint List has offered to support a government from outside without joining it.

### What are Eisenkot's four conditions for coalition partners?

That Israel is a Jewish, democratic, liberal and egalitarian state under its Declaration of Independence; that every citizen does military, national or civilian service; that Hamas is recognised as a terrorist organisation; and that Hamas's military and governing power in Gaza is destroyed. Any member of Knesset who accepts them, he says, can be a partner.

### Was the Israeli election stolen?

There is no evidence that it was. Likud said the Arab parties "stole" the election with foreign money, resting on the arrest of three Ra'am activists in one city. The police said there was no investigative or intelligence basis for the claim, and the Central Elections Committee said the vote was lawful. A limited Shin Bet inquiry into suspected foreign funding, now assisted by the Mossad, is continuing.

### What happens after the Israeli election?

The Central Elections Committee hands the official result to President Herzog on 4 November. He then consults the parties and asks the candidate most likely to form a coalition to try, with 28 days and a possible 14-day extension. The Knesset is sworn in on 10 November. If nobody succeeds, Israel could face another election.

### How should Christians pray about Israel's election?

Pray for all in authority (1 Timothy 2:1-2) and for the peace of Jerusalem (Psalm 122:6): for whoever forms the government, for the bereaved and the hostages' families, for Arab and Jewish believers in Israel, and with confidence that God's purposes for Israel do not depend on the outcome (Romans 11:29).
