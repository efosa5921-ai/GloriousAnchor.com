Israelis voted on Tuesday 27 October 2026 in their first general election since the Hamas attack of 7 October 2023, and in record numbers. Nobody won. The final count gives Benjamin Netanyahu's Likud 27 seats and his bloc 53, eight short of a majority; the bloc led by former army chief Gadi Eisenkot has 48; and the two Arab lists hold a record 19. On Wednesday 4 November the Central Elections Committee certified the result, finding no evidence of fraud, and President Isaac Herzog spent two days hearing the parties. On Thursday night he announced the count: factions holding 67 seats recommended Eisenkot and 46 recommended Netanyahu. Then he said the numbers did not settle it, and that he would name his candidate on Sunday 8 November.

This article gives the final result, the counts and exit polls it replaced, where the coalition talks stand, and how Christians might pray. We keep updating it as the talks move.

**Update, Sunday 8 November, 8 pm Israel time.** At five o'clock this afternoon, at the President's Residence in Jerusalem, Isaac Herzog handed Gadi Eisenkot the letter tasking him with forming Israel's next government. The president said the consultations had made it "evident that Mr. Eisenkot has received the clear and unequivocal support of a majority of Members of Knesset on the most central question: who has the better chance of forming the next government." He acknowledged that Likud is the largest party, and said precedent allowed him to pass over it. Eisenkot has 28 days, to Sunday 6 December, with a possible 14-day extension to 20 December. He answered with one line on X: "Now is the time to form a national emergency government. I will do everything to ensure we are worthy." Netanyahu, in a recorded message on Saturday before the decision was announced, called a government backed by the Arab parties "national suicide" and offered a unity government that would include every party of his outgoing coalition; Yashar called the speech "hysterical" and said Likud is welcome without its "extremists". Liberman backs Eisenkot's bid but will not sit in a government dependent on Ra'am or the Joint List; Bennett said "we have no time"; Golan said the Democrats are "not looking to punish the ultra-Orthodox" and want a partnership of "mutual responsibility" with them. Ben Gvir wants new elections. Aryeh Deri has withdrawn from the Shas list and will not sit in the 26th Knesset, though he stays party chairman. Both of the Shin Bet's interference probes involving Arab parties are reported to be falling apart. The details are below.

**Update, Friday 6 November.** The consultations are over and the arithmetic is as Ynet forecast. Herzog announced on Thursday evening that "representatives of factions comprising 67 Knesset members recommended Yashar party chairman Gadi Eisenkot, while representatives of factions comprising 46 Knesset members recommended Prime Minister and Likud chairman Benjamin Netanyahu." Together, the Democrats, Yisrael Beytenu, Ra'am and the Joint List all backed Eisenkot; Likud, Otzma Yehudit, Shas and Religious Zionism backed Netanyahu; United Torah Judaism, with seven seats, recommended nobody. It is the largest number of recommendations any candidate has received since 2015. But Herzog refused to treat the count as the answer: "The results of the consultations may appear clear, but the authority vested in me by law is to determine who has the ability to form a government, and that is not entirely clear, given the mutual vetoes between the blocs and within the blocs themselves." He will talk to party leaders one by one over the weekend and "leave no stone unturned", and name his choice on Sunday. Yashar called Netanyahu "a caretaker prime minister with a minority of just 46 seats" with "no mandate, no majority, and no confidence from the people" and asked for the mandate at once, to form "a national emergency government". Likud, the last party in on Thursday night, told the president the largest party should get it, and its Yoav Kisch warned him against a government dependent on the Arab parties. The Shin Bet, after days of refusing, has begun handing its election-interference evidence to the police, and its chief has undertaken to follow the attorney general's directions. The details are below.

**Update, Wednesday 4 November, 8 pm Israel time.** The committee's chairman, Justice Noam Sohlberg, handed President Herzog the certified result this morning after a review that "found no evidence of irregularities that compromised the integrity of the election or affected its results"; Likud abstained on the committee vote and Otzma Yehudit voted against. Herzog said there was "no clear winner", told the parties to "stop climbing trees" and come down with open minds, and backed the Shin Bet's inquiry into foreign money, which the agency now says involved "large sums" from parties with "hidden and even hostile interests". Then the consultations began, smallest parties first. Religious Zionism and Shas recommended Netanyahu; Yisrael Beytenu recommended Eisenkot, settling the question Liberman had left open; Ra'am will recommend Eisenkot when it meets Herzog at a quarter past twelve on Thursday. The Joint List is expected to do the same, and United Torah Judaism is expected to recommend nobody. On those expectations Eisenkot has 48 recommendations from his own bloc to Netanyahu's 46, and up to 67 with the Arab lists. Netanyahu broke his silence in a recorded video on Wednesday evening, calling for "a broad national government" under his own leadership "without terror supporters" and demanding a full investigation of foreign interference; the Shin Bet's chief, meanwhile, has asked the attorney general to question three Arab party leaders under caution, and she has accused him of subverting the rule of law. Herzog hears the remaining parties on Thursday, Likud last at 8.30 pm, and is expected to name his candidate between Friday and Tuesday. The details are below.

## The final result, party by party

These are the Central Elections Committee's final results, published early on Friday 30 October after the double-envelope count, set beside the partial count of the regular polling stations from Wednesday night and the two main exit polls from election night. The blocs: Netanyahu's coalition bloc (Likud, Shas, United Torah Judaism, Otzma Yehudit, Religious Zionism) 53; Eisenkot's change bloc (Yashar, Together, the Democrats, Yisrael Beytenu) 48; the Arab lists (Joint List and Ra'am) 19. The soldiers' and absentee ballots, which have swung seats in past elections, moved only two this time, and both within a bloc: one from Shas to Likud and one from the Joint List to Ra'am. The committee says the figures are still subject to its integrity checks and "minor adjustments," and they become official when they are handed to the president on 4 November.

Three things stand out. The exit polls had Likud about right in the end but, as the networks themselves admitted overnight, overstated the change bloc. Shas, which went into the election with 11 seats, came out with 6, its lowest since 1992. And the Arab parties have done better than any poll predicted: nearly 840,000 people voted for the Joint List and Ra'am, 64 per cent more than in 2022, on a turnout in Arab towns of about 80 per cent, and 19 seats is the most they have ever held.

## How the count moved

Exit polls in Israel are taken until a couple of hours before the polls close, and the networks revise them as late samples come in. On Tuesday night the revisions ran one way. Channel 12 went from 50 for Netanyahu's bloc and 52 for Eisenkot's to 52 and 49, with the Joint List and Ra'am together on 19. Channel 13 moved the Arab parties up to 18 and Eisenkot's bloc down to 51. Channel 14's late update gave Netanyahu 61, a majority of one, but only by assuming Avigdor Liberman's party missed the threshold.

Then the real count began. At 2.30 am the Central Elections Committee said it had counted its first 100,000 ballots: 27 per cent for Likud, 17 per cent for Yashar, 9 per cent for Together. By 37 per cent counted the coalition bloc was on 57; at 55 per cent it was 56 to 49; at 66 per cent, 54 to 48; at 77.6 per cent, 54 to 49; at 88.6 per cent, with the regular stations all but done on Wednesday night, 53 to 48, where it stayed. The double envelopes were counted on Thursday and the final result was published at about half past midnight on Friday.

## A record turnout

By eight in the evening turnout stood at 74.3 per cent, against 66.3 per cent at the same hour last time, and the final figure was 77.9 per cent, the highest since 1999 and within a point of the all-time record of 1973. Turnout in Arab towns was among the highest in the country. Among Israelis voting at embassies abroad it was 86 per cent. Whatever else the night showed, Israelis wanted to be heard.

## What the two leaders said on election night

Gadi Eisenkot, the former chief of staff who entered politics after losing a son in Gaza, wrote on X: "If these are the final results, this is a victory for Israel's responsible Zionist majority," and promised a government "committed to universal conscription as soon as possible." Naftali Bennett told his supporters after midnight: "The alliance of those who serve has defeated the alliance of draft dodgers. Netanyahu has lost, and the October 7 government is going home." Yair Lapid said "the State of Israel saved itself today" and that the opposition, as the side with more votes, had the duty to form a government. Eisenkot told his party's headquarters later: "This will be a new start. A real new start on a new path. The old era is over, and Israel is starting a new era."

Netanyahu claimed the opposite. "What we've seen is that the Likud party is the largest party. Nobody can compete with us," he said. "The second thing that is clear as well is that the right bloc has a majority. And it is a decisive majority." He called for a "national right-wing government" and accused the television channels of projecting stronger opposition results to discourage his voters. Before the polls closed he had posted a video calling the high turnout in Arab towns part of an effort to "steal" the election. The count did not bear out the "decisive majority": his bloc finished eight seats short.

## The "stolen election" claim, and what the police found

On Wednesday Likud went further than its leader. In a statement it said the Arab parties had "stolen" the election through fraud, bribery, intimidation and ballot manipulation, with foreign money behind them, and called the result "an attack on democracy aimed at enabling a foreign takeover of the Knesset." The claim rested on the arrest of three Ra'am activists suspected of paying a small number of voters in one Arab city.

The answer came quickly. A police source said there was "no investigative- or intelligence-based supporting evidence for the claims." The director of the Central Elections Committee rejected the talk of widespread fraud as baseless, and the committee said the election had been conducted lawfully and there was no basis for claims of systematic interference with the integrity of the vote. Its chairman, Justice Noam Sohlberg, did ask the Shin Bet to examine the separate suspicion that foreign governments, Qatar first among them, had funded parts of the Arab campaign. The Shin Bet described that inquiry as "limited," and a source familiar with it told Haaretz that any effect on the result was probably minimal. On Friday the Mossad agreed, at the Shin Bet's request, to help with the foreign side of the inquiry; Ynet reported that Turkey was also being looked at. Qatar rejected the allegations as a disinformation campaign.

Eisenkot accused Netanyahu of trying to delegitimise the election. Netanyahu, he said, was now a caretaker prime minister "without a mandate and without public confidence," and he warned that the inquiry must not be used to delay the publication of the results or the swearing-in of the Knesset, from which he would not allow "a single centimetre" of deviation. He promised that his government would set up a state commission of inquiry into 7 October and pass a conscription law, and said he would back military action before a new government is formed only against direct and immediate threats.

## The coalition talks: Eisenkot's four conditions

Eisenkot spent the campaign ruling out a government that depends on the Arab parties, and three of the four parties in his bloc pledged the same. On Saturday night he moved. In a statement he said the record turnout and the challenges facing Israel demanded "an extraordinary effort" to avoid another election and a long political crisis, and that any member of Knesset who accepts four principles can be a partner in forming the next government, whatever party or bloc they come from. The four: Israel as a Jewish, democratic, liberal and egalitarian state, under its Declaration of Independence; military, national or civilian service for every citizen; recognition of Hamas as a terrorist organisation; and the destruction of Hamas's military and governing power in Gaza.

The door is open a crack, not wide. Eisenkot still says he does not want a government dependent on the Arab lists, and the Joint List's leaders say they do not want to be in one. Yousef Jabareen said the Joint List would support Eisenkot in the Knesset without joining his government, and Ahmad Tibi said the list would back replacing the Knesset speaker but ruled out ministries. Both the Joint List and Ra'am are expected to recommend Eisenkot to the president, and Mansour Abbas has said he is willing to repeat his 2021 partnership. On those assumptions Ynet counts 60 recommendations for Eisenkot (his bloc without Liberman, plus the 19 Arab seats) against 46 for Netanyahu (his bloc without United Torah Judaism), with Liberman's 7 and United Torah Judaism's 7 undecided. Sixty is one short of a majority, and recommendations are not a coalition.

Netanyahu answered the same evening on X: "Cooperation with Mansour Abbas and Tibi is a disaster for Israel." Likud accused Eisenkot of intending to cooperate with "parties that support terrorism" and called his statement "hysterical." Netanyahu's allies have been floating the other way out, a unity government with Eisenkot and a rotating premiership, which Eisenkot has refused again and again; a week before the vote he said that if he found himself in a rotation with Netanyahu "you would see me resign an hour later."

## Wednesday: the result is certified and the consultations begin

The day began at the Central Elections Committee, which approved the final result despite Likud abstaining and Otzma Yehudit voting against. Its review, the committee said, "found no evidence of irregularities that compromised the integrity of the election or affected its results." Justice Sohlberg then took the result to the President's Residence, where Herzog accepted it as legally binding and said: "I place my full confidence in the work of the Shin Bet, the police and law enforcement agencies. Every suspicion of subversive external influence on election results must be investigated thoroughly." The Shin Bet's update to the committee, reported the same morning, speaks of "widespread phenomena of large sums of money flowing into Israel from foreign countries, originating from parties with hidden and even hostile interests." Whether any of it reached the ballot box is the question the inquiry has yet to answer; the committee's finding is that it did not change the result.

Netanyahu's answer, the night before, was given at a meeting of his bloc at the Prime Minister's Office at which, according to Israeli reports, his wife Sara took a leading part: "They're robbing the country. Stealing our elections. There are foreign countries here that have penetrated and are stealing our sovereignty." Likud has mooted refusing to recognise the result. In public he said that "a clear majority in the Jewish public" had chosen Likud as the largest party and him as prime minister, and that he was open to talks.

Herzog broke with tradition and heard the smallest parties first, live on television. Religious Zionism, with six seats, came first and recommended Netanyahu, calling for "a broad Jewish government"; its delegation of Orit Strook, Itamar Ben-Gvir and Tzvi Succot told the president that the Arab parties were not legitimate partners "because they are nationalist" and because they call Israel's founding a "Nakba". Herzog pushed back: "How do you distinguish between racism and what you're saying about not sitting with Arabs?" Strook replied: "I think reality made the distinction." Herzog pointed to Ra'am's number two, Yoav Segalovitz, a former senior police officer. "We need to understand that we live with Arab citizens in Israel," he said. "This generalisation really bothers me."

Shas, down from eleven seats to six, recommended Netanyahu after he pledged to keep working on a law regulating yeshiva students' status. Then came the day's news: Avigdor Liberman's Yisrael Beytenu, which had spent the week refusing to say, recommended Eisenkot. Ra'am announced that it will do the same when it meets Herzog at 12.15 pm on Thursday, and the Joint List is expected to follow, though as of Wednesday night it had not formally said so. United Torah Judaism is expected to recommend nobody. Yair Lapid said there was "no possibility" of a unity government under Netanyahu. The remaining factions, including Likud and Yashar, see the president on Thursday.

Two more things happened on Wednesday. Netanyahu issued his first statement since election night, a recorded video, calling for "a broad national government" under his leadership and "without terror supporters", warning against any government that relies on "Islamist parties that support terrorism", and demanding a full inquiry into foreign interference; Channel 14 reported that he has concluded Eisenkot will not serve under him even in rotation and has given up on unity talks. And the foreign-money inquiry turned into a constitutional row. The Shin Bet's director, David Zini, asked the attorney general to question Mansour Abbas, Ahmad Tibi and Sami Abu Shahadeh under caution, along with a fourth member of Knesset over alleged Palestinian Authority funds, while telling the elections committee the suspected funding "may involve thousands of people"; Attorney General Gali Baharav-Miara replied that he had provided evidence on only the fourth, accused him of subverting the rule of law and demanded to see the rest. Itamar Ben-Gvir called for all the Arab party leaders to be arrested; Ayman Odeh, reported to be among those suspected, called it political persecution. Herzog, who had asked the parties for "originality, creativity, boldness and goodwill", also warned Netanyahu that rising Jewish violence in the West Bank could lead to civil war. Yisrael Beytenu's Oded Forer told the president that his party would not sit with the Arab parties but did not rule out Likud, and Liberman is said to back Yair Lapid for Knesset Speaker.

Thursday's order is United Torah Judaism at 8.30 am, then Otzma Yehudit, the Democrats, Ra'am, the Joint List, Together and Yashar, with Likud last at 8.30 pm, after which Herzog may announce his choice; the mandate is expected between Friday and Tuesday.

The arithmetic of recommendations, then: Eisenkot 48 from Yashar, Together, the Democrats and Yisrael Beytenu; Netanyahu 46 from Likud, Shas, Otzma Yehudit and Religious Zionism; Ra'am's 8 and the Joint List's 11 taking Eisenkot to 67 if both follow through; United Torah Judaism's 7 abstaining. Herzog is not bound to follow the count, only to pick the member of Knesset most likely to assemble a government, and his office says he has not ruled out a second round of consultations. But a 67-to-46 count would be hard to set aside.

## Thursday: 67 to 46, and a president who will not just count

Thursday's consultations ran from morning to night in the order Herzog had set, smallest to largest. United Torah Judaism came first and, as expected, recommended nobody, keeping its seven seats in reserve for whoever can promise a conscription law it can live with. Otzma Yehudit recommended Netanyahu. The Democrats, Ra'am, the Joint List, Together and Yashar recommended Eisenkot; the Joint List's Ahmad Tibi had said in advance that his list would back "a candidate from the anti-Netanyahu Zionist parties", and so it did. Likud came last, at half past eight, and recommended Netanyahu; Miri Regev argued that the mandate belongs to the leader of the largest party, Yoav Kisch warned the president against any government that depends on the Arab parties, and Herzog, who had been sharp with the Religious Zionists the day before, told Regev to keep "a little perspective".

Then he gave the count, 67 to 46, and made clear that it would not decide him on its own. "I asked difficult questions in an effort to help the parties and the public to break free from set ways of thinking and open their minds to other paths," he said of the two days. The law asks him to name the member of Knesset most likely to form a government, and "that is not entirely clear, given the mutual vetoes between the blocs and within the blocs themselves." Which vetoes he meant, everyone knew: Eisenkot, Bennett and Liberman will not depend on the Arab parties whose 19 recommendations give Eisenkot his majority; Liberman will not sit with the ultra-Orthodox or under Netanyahu; Lapid and Eisenkot will not sit under Netanyahu at all; and Netanyahu will not sit under anyone. Herzog said he would spend the days before Sunday talking to the leaders himself and would "leave no stone unturned in an effort to reach out and build a stable and good government for the people of Israel."

Yashar's answer was impatient. The party said Eisenkot had received more recommendations than any candidate since 2015, that "Israeli citizens voted for change and expressed a complete lack of confidence in outgoing Prime Minister Netanyahu and his government", and that the president should hand over the mandate at once so that Eisenkot could form "a national emergency government" for Israel's security and recovery. "All public leaders who care about Israel's future must now rise to the occasion," it said, "set aside narrow political and personal considerations, and ensure that a government is formed as quickly as possible," warning that failure meant another election "and a heavy cost on the country."

The foreign-money inquiry moved too. After the attorney general's rebuke, the Shin Bet, the police and the State Attorney's Office met for the first time on the case, the Shin Bet began transferring its evidence to the police, and its director undertook to act on the attorney general's directions. The attorney general's position stands: one Knesset member may be questioned under caution, on evidence of receiving Palestinian Authority funds; for the three Arab party leaders the Shin Bet named, no evidence has yet been produced.

## Sunday: Herzog hands Eisenkot the letter

The ceremony was short and the president's speech was not. Herzog set out the count again, 67 to 46 with seven abstaining, and then the reasoning: "I am well aware that Likud is the largest faction in the incoming Knesset. I noted this during the consultations as well. However, both on the basis of the facts I have set out, and on the basis of clear precedents set by my predecessors, who in several cases tasked the leader of a party that was not the largest with forming the government, I have decided to task Gadi Eisenkot with forming a government."

He listed what the next government must face: Iran, "the Board of Peace and President Trump's twenty-point plan for Gaza", negotiations with Lebanon, sanctions and "a new political situation in the United States" after the midterms, the question of military service, the boundaries between the branches of government, crime in Arab society, the economy, and "the wound of October 7th, which remains open in our flesh." Then he turned to the man in front of him: "Your face bears the scars of bullets from decades of command and combat... and your soul bears the most terrible scar of all," the death of Eisenkot's son Gal and two nephews in the war. "The eyes of the entire nation, with all its pain, hopes, prayers, and fears, are upon you."

Eisenkot said he would send formal invitations to every Knesset faction, that any partner must sign his four principles, and that the government would be built on the "four Zionist parties" of the change bloc. That is 48 seats. The arithmetic has not moved since election night.

The reactions had come in on Saturday, after Herzog's office announced his intention. Netanyahu's recorded message warned that "as early as next week, there are those who intend to hastily swear in a government with supporters of terrorism," that such a government would "dismantle the State of Israel from within, as is happening in European countries, and much worse," and that "there is only one choice: not national suicide, but national unity." Yashar's reply: "The man who divided, tore apart and sowed discord in Israeli society is heard saying the word 'unity' only when he realizes his days in office are numbered." Liberman gave Eisenkot his full backing for the attempt and repeated that he will not support a government that depends on Ra'am or the Joint List, while leaving a door open to the Haredi parties. Golan, from the other end of the bloc, moved toward the same door: "We are not looking to punish the ultra-Orthodox," he said, asking for conscription and workforce integration in return and adding that in secular towns "it should be possible to go out for a night on the town on Shabbat." Bennett: "We have no time."

On the right, Ben Gvir demanded fresh elections over his unproven claim that the result was manipulated, and said Justice Sohlberg "was wrong" to certify it; he also opposed Netanyahu's unity offer. Aryeh Deri, whose Shas fell from eleven seats to six, has withdrawn from the party's list and will not sit in the new Knesset, though the party's Council of Torah Sages refused his resignation as chairman. Two other Shas candidates also stepped off the list. And the two Shin Bet investigations into Arab party leaders, which a week ago were the government's chief argument against the result, are reported to be collapsing: the attorney general has refused to let anyone be questioned without evidence, the agency has handed over little, and a police source described "a huge gap" between the Shin Bet chief's claims and what he has produced.

## Liberman walks away from the phone

The man who could decide it is Avigdor Liberman, whose seven seats would give Eisenkot and the Arab parties 67 or Netanyahu's bloc 60. On Thursday he told his party's activists: "We will not sit with Bibi or with Tibi." Channel 12 then reported that he had largely stopped answering calls from Eisenkot and Bennett, with only their staff still in touch, and that his view is that if anyone from the change bloc goes into a unity government with Netanyahu it should be Eisenkot, not him. Liberman denied a report that he is angling for the Knesset speaker's chair. On Wednesday he answered the question in the only way that counts: his party recommended Eisenkot to the president. Eisenkot and Bennett, meeting at Bennett's home in Ra'anana, issued a joint statement promising to work "to establish a good government in Israel as quickly as possible." Lapid's verdict on the week was shorter: "A win is a win."

## The street

On Saturday night thousands filled Habima Square in Tel Aviv to urge Eisenkot and his partners to make the deal with the Arab parties. Protesters told AFP this was the "last chance for real change" and called Netanyahu "a great manipulator." Netanyahu's supporters held a counter-protest, chanting that Israel should not have a government "with terrorists," and right-wing activists planned a demonstration outside Eisenkot's home. The Tel Aviv stock market, which fell about 1.5 per cent on the morning after the vote, is waiting like everyone else.

## What happens next

Eisenkot's 28 days run to Sunday 6 December. Herzog can add up to 14 days, to 20 December. If he fails, the president can hand the task to someone else, most likely Netanyahu, for another 28 days; if that fails, a majority of the Knesset can nominate a candidate within 21 days, and if nobody is sworn in by the end of that road Israel votes again, a sixth election in seven years. The 26th Knesset is sworn in on Tuesday 10 November. Netanyahu stays prime minister until a new government wins the Knesset's confidence.

Eisenkot has the letter and 48 seats. The roads to 61 are the same four, and each still has someone standing in it. A minority government of the four Zionist parties, kept in office by Ra'am and the Joint List from outside, is what his four principles were written to allow and what Liberman says he will not join; Mansour Abbas, for his part, wants at least one Ra'am member inside. A government with the Haredi parties, 48 plus 13 for exactly 61, is the road both Liberman and Golan edged toward this weekend, and it runs through the conscription law United Torah Judaism has refused to let anyone pass. A unity government with Likud is on offer from both sides, but Netanyahu's version includes Ben Gvir and Smotrich and Eisenkot's version excludes Netanyahu. And the sixth election is the road nobody wants and everybody is warning about.

## Why the result was so close

This was the first election since 7 October 2023 and the two-year war that followed in Gaza, Lebanon, Iran and beyond. Netanyahu's government was the first to serve a full term since 1973, but it did so through mass protests over judicial reform, an International Criminal Court warrant for its prime minister, and the collapse of its relationship with the ultra-Orthodox parties, which left the coalition in 2025 over military conscription for yeshiva students. Eisenkot, who sat in the war cabinet after 7 October, built his campaign on competence and accountability for that day. The voters split almost exactly down the middle, and then the Arab citizens of Israel, voting in numbers they never had before, made the split a deadlock.

## Questions this article answers

### Who won the Israel election on 27 October 2026?

Nobody. The certified result gives Benjamin Netanyahu's bloc 53 seats and Gadi Eisenkot's 48, both short of the 61 needed; the Arab parties hold a record 19. Likud is the largest party with 27 seats. President Herzog began consulting the parties on 4 November and must name a candidate to form a government by 11 November.

### Who did Herzog task with forming Israel's government?

Gadi Eisenkot, leader of the Yashar party and a former IDF chief of staff, at a ceremony at the President's Residence on Sunday 8 November 2026. Herzog said Eisenkot had "the clear and unequivocal support of a majority of Members of Knesset" and that precedent allowed him to pass over Likud, the largest party. Eisenkot's own bloc holds 48 seats, so he must still find 13 more.

### Who did the parties recommend to President Herzog?

Factions holding 67 seats recommended Eisenkot: Yashar, Together, the Democrats, Yisrael Beytenu, Ra'am and the Joint List. Factions holding 46 recommended Netanyahu: Likud, Shas, Otzma Yehudit and Religious Zionism. United Torah Judaism, with seven seats, recommended nobody. Herzog announced the count on Thursday 5 November and said he would name his candidate on Sunday 8 November.

### How many seats did Likud and Yashar win?

Likud won 27 seats and Eisenkot's Yashar 21, with Together, led by Naftali Bennett and Yair Lapid, on 12. The exit polls had put Likud at 24 to 30 and Yashar at 19 to 23. Shas fell to 6, its lowest since 1992.

### How many seats did the Arab parties win?

A record 19: 11 for the Joint List and 8 for Ra'am, on nearly 840,000 votes, 64 per cent more than in 2022. Neither main bloc can reach 61 without them or without a unity deal. Both are expected to recommend Eisenkot to the president, and the Joint List has offered to support a government from outside without joining it.

### What are Eisenkot's four conditions for coalition partners?

That Israel is a Jewish, democratic, liberal and egalitarian state under its Declaration of Independence; that every citizen does military, national or civilian service; that Hamas is recognised as a terrorist organisation; and that Hamas's military and governing power in Gaza is destroyed. Any member of Knesset who accepts them, he says, can be a partner.

### Was the Israeli election stolen?

The Central Elections Committee certified the result on 4 November, finding "no evidence of irregularities that compromised the integrity of the election or affected its results." Likud said the Arab parties "stole" the election with foreign money, resting on the arrest of three Ra'am activists in one city. The police said there was no investigative or intelligence basis for the claim, and the Central Elections Committee said the vote was lawful. A limited Shin Bet inquiry into suspected foreign funding, now assisted by the Mossad, is continuing.

### What happens after the Israeli election?

Herzog tasked Gadi Eisenkot with forming the government on Sunday 8 November, after 67 Knesset members recommended him and 46 recommended Netanyahu. Eisenkot has until 6 December, with a possible extension to 20 December. The Knesset is sworn in on 10 November, and Netanyahu remains prime minister until a new government is sworn in. If Eisenkot fails, Netanyahu may get a turn, and if nobody succeeds Israel votes again.
