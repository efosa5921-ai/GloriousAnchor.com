> *He changes times and seasons; he removes kings and sets up kings.* (Daniel 2:21)

Americans voted on Tuesday 3 November 2026 and handed both houses of Congress to the Democrats, two years after giving Donald Trump a second term with a Republican Senate and House. The Associated Press called the Senate for the Democrats late on election night and the House in the early hours of Wednesday. The networks are calling it a rout, and the exit polls say what it was about: the president. Nearly half of voters said their vote was partly a rebuke of him, and his approval among those who voted, 38 per cent, is the lowest recorded for any president at a midterm.

This article gives the results as they stand on Wednesday morning, with some races still being counted, then looks at the Christian vote and at what a Democratic Congress means for two things this site follows closely: the persecution of Christians in Nigeria, and America's relationship with Israel.

## The Senate: seven seats flipped

Republicans went into the night with 53 seats to 47 and the Democrats needed a net gain of four. They gained seven. NBC's count on Wednesday morning stood at 54 Democrats to 44 Republicans, with Alaska and Mississippi still too close to call.

Texas and Kansas sending Democrats to the Senate, and Maine and South Carolina removing two of the chamber's longest-serving Republicans, are the measure of the night.

## The House: Jeffries in line to be Speaker

Democrats needed a net gain of three seats in the House. By Wednesday morning NBC had 218 seats confirmed for them, the majority line, and projected they would finish with at least 244 of the 435, with dozens of races still to be called. Several of the seats they won had been redrawn by Republican legislatures to keep them Republican. Hakeem Jeffries of New York is expected to be elected Speaker when the new Congress meets in January, replacing Mike Johnson.

In the governors' races, Josh Shapiro was re-elected in Pennsylvania, J.B. Pritzker in Illinois and Katie Hobbs in Arizona, Jocelyn Benson won Michigan, and Amy Klobuchar became the first woman elected governor of Minnesota.

## What Trump said

The president's first reaction, as the polls closed and the early returns came in, was to turn on Fox News, which he said was "way down, not because of me, but because they are AGAINST me!" By morning he had settled on a line. He "wasn't on the ballot", he wrote on Truth Social, and if he had been he would have won "in a landslide". He had no regrets about his endorsements, he said, would "absolutely" accept the results, congratulated the Democrats and said he was willing to work with them. He also called Greg Abbott, the Republican governor of Texas, a "RINO".

The exit polls tell a harder story. NBC found 62 per cent of voters disapproving of the president, 59 per cent saying he was a factor in their vote, and 43 per cent saying one reason for their vote was to oppose him. The economy was the first concern of most voters, and most of those blamed the administration.

## How Christians voted

The full exit-poll breakdown by religion will take a day or two, but the shape of it was visible before the vote. Pew Research's survey in early October found white evangelical Protestants still the most Republican group in the country, 73 per cent intending to vote for the Republican House candidate against 12 per cent for the Democrat, and still the only religious group in which more people approved of the president than disapproved. White Catholics leaned Republican by 50 to 32; Hispanic Catholics leaned Democratic by 59 to 19.

But the evangelical wall had cracks. Three polls through October showed double-digit falls in the president's approval among white evangelicals since the start of the year, and an NPR survey in late October found only 61 per cent of them "very enthusiastic" about voting, behind self-described MAGA voters at 68. Conservative groups spent the last fortnight trying to turn out the "low propensity" evangelicals who vote only in presidential years, and admitted the campaign was not being fought on the issues that most move them. On the night, the Republican coalition did not hold in Texas, Ohio, Iowa, Kansas or the Carolinas, all states with large evangelical populations. The evangelicals who did vote stayed loyal; too many stayed home, and everyone else moved.

## What it means for Nigeria's Christians

The American pressure on Nigeria over the killing of Christians, which this site reported [last week](/articles/media-silence-christians-murdered-nigeria/), has been a Republican project: Senator Ted Cruz's bill, Congresswoman Nancy Mace's campaign, Chris Smith's Nigeria Religious Freedom and Accountability Act, and President Trump's designation of Nigeria as a Country of Particular Concern on 31 October 2025, followed by the Christmas Day strikes on Islamic State positions.

The designation and the military cooperation are the executive's and do not change with Congress. The legislation does. Smith's bill, introduced in February with Republican cosponsors, would require the State Department to report on persecution in Nigeria and authorise sanctions on those responsible; it has not reached the floor, and from January its fate is in Democratic hands. Religious freedom abroad has long had bipartisan support in Congress, and the new chairs of the foreign affairs committees will decide whether Nigeria stays on the agenda or is filed, as much of the press has filed it, under Republican culture war. Christians who want the pressure kept up now have Democratic members to write to as well as Republican ones.

## What it means for Israel

The two men who will run Congress, Hakeem Jeffries and Chuck Schumer, are lifelong supporters of Israel; Schumer was one of only seven Senate Democrats who voted in April against resolutions to block arms sales to it. But they lead a party that has moved. A Quinnipiac poll last year found two thirds of Democrats opposed to sending Israel more military aid; almost half the House Democratic caucus voted in July for an amendment to halt it, which Jeffries opposed while signalling openness to conditions; and the Block the Bombs bill has gathered well over a hundred cosponsors. Michigan's new senator, Abdul El-Sayed, said during the campaign that Israel is "not an ally".

The next ten-year memorandum of understanding on American military aid to Israel is due to be negotiated during the Congress that has just been elected. The Jewish Telegraphic Agency's verdict is that a Democratic Congress "could push the US further away from Israel", even as the party's leaders try to hold the line. With Israel itself in a post-election deadlock, which we are following [here](/articles/israel-election-results-exit-polls/), the two democracies most bound to each other both begin a winter of coalition arithmetic.

## How a Christian should read it

Scripture is clear about who sets up and removes rulers (Daniel 2:21; Psalm 75:6-7), and equally clear about what believers owe the rulers they get: "supplications, prayers, intercessions, and thanksgivings... for kings and all who are in high positions, that we may lead a peaceful and quiet life, godly and dignified in every way" (1 Timothy 2:1-2). Paul wrote that under Nero. It covers a Democratic Congress and a Republican president, and it covers the Christians who voted for each.

So pray for the new Congress and for the president who must work with it; for the persecuted in Nigeria, that their cause does not become a casualty of Washington's change of hands; for Israel; and for the American church, whose witness depends less on who won on Tuesday than on how it treats those who lost. "Put not your trust in princes" (Psalm 146:3) was never a counsel of despair. It is a reminder of where the trust goes.

## Questions this article answers

### Who won the 2026 US midterm elections?

The Democrats. They won the Senate, 54 seats to 44 with two still being counted, flipping seven Republican seats, and the House, where NBC projects at least 244 of 435 seats. Hakeem Jeffries is expected to become Speaker in January.

### Which Senate seats flipped in 2026?

North Carolina (Roy Cooper), Ohio (Sherrod Brown), Texas (James Talarico over Ken Paxton), Maine (Troy Jackson over Susan Collins), Iowa (Josh Turek), Kansas (Adam Hamilton) and South Carolina (Annie Andrews over Lindsey Graham), all from Republican to Democrat.

### How did evangelicals vote in the 2026 midterms?

Pre-election polling by Pew had white evangelicals backing Republican House candidates 73 to 12 per cent, still the most Republican group in America, but with double-digit falls in the president's approval among them during 2026 and lower enthusiasm than in presidential years. Full exit-poll breakdowns by religion are still to come.

### What did Trump say about the midterm results?

That he "wasn't on the ballot" and would have won "in a landslide" if he had been, that he had no regrets about his endorsements, and that he would accept the results and work with the Democrats. He also attacked Fox News and called Texas Governor Greg Abbott a "RINO".

### What do the midterms mean for Christians in Nigeria?

Nigeria's designation as a Country of Particular Concern and the US military cooperation are executive decisions and stand. The Nigeria Religious Freedom and Accountability Act, a Republican bill authorising sanctions on persecutors, now depends on Democratic committee chairs to advance.

### What do the midterms mean for Israel?

Congress's new leaders, Jeffries and Schumer, support Israel, but their party increasingly favours conditioning or cutting military aid, and the next ten-year aid agreement will be negotiated by this Congress. Expect more pressure on arms sales and on the terms of aid.
