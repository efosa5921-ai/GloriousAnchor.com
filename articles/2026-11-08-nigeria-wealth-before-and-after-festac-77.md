> *For she did not know that I gave her corn, and wine, and oil, and multiplied her silver and gold, which they prepared for Baal.* (Hosea 2:8)

In January 1977, the month FESTAC opened, one naira bought about a dollar and fifty-five cents. Three years earlier Nigeria had lent the International Monetary Fund 120 million dollars and the World Bank 240 million more, because it had reserves it could not spend fast enough. The head of state had said, in words every Nigerian over sixty can quote, that money was not the country's problem but how to spend it.

In June 2026 the Debt Management Office valued Nigeria's debts at 166.79 trillion naira, about 121 billion dollars, converting at 1,379 naira to the dollar. Nigeria owes the World Bank more than twenty billion dollars. One naira now buys a little under a fifteenth of a cent.

This article sets the two Nigerias side by side: what the nation had before 1977, what it did with it, what happened after, and what Scripture says about a people who use God's gifts to honour other gods. It is a companion to our article on FESTAC itself and the acts performed there. The figures below are from the IMF, the World Bank, the Debt Management Office and the historians of the period; where sources disagree the range is given.

## Before oil: the land fed the nation and paid for it

Nigeria at independence in 1960 was an agricultural power. Agriculture produced more than six tenths of national output and around seven tenths of export earnings. The country was the world's largest exporter of palm oil and palm kernels into the mid-1960s, with something like four tenths of world trade. It was the world's largest exporter of groundnuts, and the sacks of the northern harvest were stacked into the pyramids of Kano that stood as tall as houses and were photographed for every school textbook. It was the second largest producer of cocoa, behind Ghana. Each region paid its own way from its own crops: cocoa in the West, groundnuts and cotton in the North, palm produce in the East. External debt in 1960 was about 150 million dollars.

Then came oil. Nigeria had exported crude since 1958, but when the Arab producers quadrupled the price in late 1973 the federal revenue leapt in a year. Between the end of the civil war in 1970 and the opening of FESTAC in 1977, the money came in faster than any government in Africa had ever had to handle.

## The boom years, 1973 to 1977

The naira was born into that boom. It replaced the pound on 1 January 1973 at a rate of about 0.66 naira to the dollar, which is to say one naira was worth a dollar and fifty cents. It was pegged there and it held. By 1980 it was stronger still, at 0.55 to the dollar, so that one naira bought a dollar and eighty cents. A Nigerian student in London in those years was rich.

In 1974 the IMF, trying to help the oil-importing countries through the shock, borrowed from the countries that had the money, and Nigeria was one of them. The former permanent secretary Abubakar Alhaji signed for 120 million dollars to the Fund's Oil Facility. The same year Nigeria lent the World Bank 240 million. For most of the period from 1973 to 1982 Nigeria stood on the creditor side of the IMF's books. Saudi princes, it is said, came to University College Hospital, Ibadan, for treatment.

The spending followed. The Udoji awards of 1975 doubled public salaries with arrears. In 1975 the government ordered something between sixteen and twenty million tonnes of cement for barracks and public works, for a port that could unload about a million tonnes a year, and four hundred ships sat at anchor off Lagos for months being paid to wait: the cement armada. And from 1975 to 1977 the nation built the National Theatre at Iganmu, FESTAC Town to house the visitors, and staged the festival itself, at a cost usually put at around 400 million dollars and by some estimates much more.

Nigeria was not in debt. On the eve of FESTAC the external debt stood at about three billion dollars, some six per cent of national output, and most of it was long-term and concessional.

## After 1977: the turn

The year after FESTAC, Nigeria went to the Eurodollar market for the first time. The one-billion-dollar "jumbo loan" signed in January 1978, for the steel projects, was followed within the year by another of 750 million. The IMF's historians date the change in Nigeria's borrowing from that year. Commercial loans, which were almost nothing in 1977, were two thirds of the debt by 1982.

Oil prices rose again in 1979 and 1980 and the Second Republic spent as if they would rise forever. Then they fell. From 1981 the oil glut cut the government's revenue by more than half; by 1982 the external debt was around 18.6 billion dollars and the Shagari government passed an austerity law. In 1983 the soldiers came back. By 1985 the debt was 19 billion and Nigeria could not pay it.

In 1986 the Babangida government took the structural adjustment programme the IMF had been pressing on it, and let the naira float. It went from under one naira to the dollar in May 1986 to two by the end of the year, four by 1988, and twenty-two by 1993. In the Abacha years it traded at over eighty on the street. It was 92 to the dollar at the return to democracy in 1999, around 150 by 2010, 305 in 2016, and after the float of 2023 it passed 1,500. The Central Bank's official rate on 30 June 2026 was 1,379. A naira that bought a dollar and a half in 1977 buys seven hundredths of a cent now.

Income fell with it. Measured in current dollars, output per Nigerian dropped from about 911 dollars in 1980 to about 262 in 1986. Measured at purchasing power, it was forty per cent lower in 2000 than in 1980. A generation grew up poorer than its parents in a country that pumped two million barrels of oil a day.

The debt did not go away. By 2004 Nigeria owed 36 billion dollars abroad, 31 billion of it to the Paris Club of rich governments, most of it interest and penalties on loans taken in the years after FESTAC. In 2005 Ngozi Okonjo-Iweala negotiated the write-off of 18 billion in return for paying 12 billion in cash, and in April 2006 the books were cleared. Within fifteen years the country had borrowed its way back. External debt was 43 billion dollars in 2023 and 54.5 billion in June 2026. Total public debt, domestic and foreign, stood at 166.79 trillion naira. A country that lent the World Bank 240 million dollars in 1974 is now one of its largest borrowers.

## What the economists say

None of this needs a spirit to explain it, and the economists have explained it. A country that lets oil pay for everything stops farming and manufacturing; by 1988 oil was 87 per cent of exports and three quarters of the government's revenue, and the palm oil and groundnut trades had collapsed. A currency held artificially strong by oil dollars makes imports cheap and the farmer's crop worthless. Military governments with no need to tax their people spent without accounting. Loans taken in boom years at floating rates came due in bust years at double the interest. Corruption took its share at every stage. This is the textbook history of the resource curse, and Nigeria is its textbook case.

All of that is true. It is also exactly what the prophets said happens.

## What the prophets say

Hosea watched a nation enjoy a boom and misread it. "For she did not know that I gave her corn, and wine, and oil, and multiplied her silver and gold, which they prepared for Baal" (Hosea 2:8). Israel's oil, in the prophet's own word, came from God, and Israel spent it on the gods of the land. The judgment that followed was not a thunderbolt. It was economic: "Therefore will I return, and take away my corn in the time thereof, and my wine in the season thereof" (2:9). The harvest failed, the trade dried up, and the people could not see why.

Moses had warned of the same mechanism before Israel ever entered the land. The danger of wealth, he said, is that "thou say in thine heart, My power and the might of mine hand hath gotten me this wealth... And it shall be, if thou do at all forget the LORD thy God, and walk after other gods, and serve them, and worship them, I testify against you this day that ye shall surely perish" (Deuteronomy 8:17–19). Haggai described a boom economy that could not hold its money: "Ye have sown much, and bring in little; ye eat, but ye have not enough... and he that earneth wages earneth wages to put it into a bag with holes" (Haggai 1:6). Every Nigerian who has watched a salary vanish between the bank and the market knows the bag with holes.

In January and February 1977, at the height of the wealth God had given it, Nigeria's government staged a festival whose opening fire was lit by a priest of Shango and whose colloquium began with a libation to the ancestors and the gods, and it paid for it with the oil money. Hosea's sentence fits it without a word changed: silver and gold, multiplied, prepared for Baal. The year after, the borrowing began. Four years after, the fall.

## Coincidence or consequence?

A Christian should be careful here, and the carefulness is itself biblical. Jesus refused to let his disciples read the tower of Siloam as a verdict on the eighteen it killed (Luke 13:4–5). The economists' explanation is real, and FESTAC did not cause the oil glut of 1981. Nigeria's rot of corruption was visible in the cement armada two years before the festival. Any honest account must say so.

But the Bible does not ask us to choose between the mechanism and the meaning. Hosea knew perfectly well that drought and Assyria were the instruments; he still said it was God taking back his corn. What a nation does with God's gifts at the height of them is, in Scripture, the thing God watches most closely. Nigeria did not merely waste its boom. It dedicated the boom, publicly, through its head of state, to spirits that are not God. The believer who says that mattered is not being superstitious. He is reading Hosea.

The hopeful part is that Hosea does not end at chapter two, verse nine. "Therefore, behold, I will allure her, and bring her into the wilderness, and speak comfortably unto her. And I will give her her vineyards from thence" (2:14–15). The God who took the corn back promises to give the vineyards again, to a people who come back to him. Nigeria's church is the largest in Africa. If the nation's fall began at an altar, its restoration can begin at one too.

## What to do with this

- **Tell the story straight.** Nigerians under fifty have never known a naira worth more than a dollar. The church should teach what the country had, what it did with it, and what Scripture says about the pattern, as history and not as legend.
- **Pray Hosea, not Siloam.** Not "whose fault was it", but "we have prepared your gifts for Baal; forgive us and give us the vineyards again."
- **Repent of the same sin in miniature.** A nation's wealth went to a festival of other gods. A Christian's wealth can go the same way, to the altars of the big man and the tithe of appearance. The judgment on national idolatry begins with the household of God.
- **Work.** Haggai's remedy for the bag with holes was not a prayer meeting but a building site: "Go up to the mountain, and bring wood, and build the house" (1:8). A repentant nation farms, makes and saves. The groundnut pyramids were built by hand.

## Questions this article answers

### Was the naira ever stronger than the dollar?

Yes. From its introduction on 1 January 1973 until 1985 the naira was pegged above the dollar: about 0.66 naira to the dollar in 1973, so one naira bought a dollar and fifty cents, and 0.55 in 1980, its strongest year. The structural adjustment devaluation of 1986 ended that. The Central Bank's official rate was 1,379 naira to the dollar on 30 June 2026.

### Did Nigeria really lend money to the IMF and the World Bank?

Yes. In 1974, under General Gowon, Nigeria lent 120 million dollars to the IMF's Oil Facility, set up to help countries hit by the oil price rise, and 240 million dollars to the World Bank. Nigeria was on the creditor side of the IMF's books for most of 1973 to 1982. It now owes the World Bank more than twenty billion dollars.

### How much was Nigeria's debt before and after FESTAC 77?

About three billion dollars before 1978, some six per cent of national output. The first one-billion-dollar commercial "jumbo loan" was signed in January 1978, the year after the festival. By 1982 external debt was around 18.6 billion dollars, by 2004 it was 36 billion, and in June 2026 total public debt was 166.79 trillion naira, about 121 billion dollars.

### Did FESTAC 77 cause Nigeria's economic decline?

Not by itself. Economists trace the fall to oil dependence, the collapse of agriculture, a currency kept artificially strong, the 1981 oil glut, military misrule and corruption, which was already visible in the cement armada of 1975. But Scripture also says what a nation does with God's gifts matters, and in 1977 Nigeria publicly dedicated its oil wealth to a festival honouring other gods. Hosea 2:8–9 describes exactly that pattern and its result.

### What does Hosea 2:8 mean for Nigeria?

Hosea says Israel did not know that its corn, wine, oil, silver and gold came from God, and used them for Baal, so God took the harvest back. Nigeria's oil wealth was spent, among other things, on a festival that honoured traditional gods and ancestors, and the decline followed. The same chapter promises that God will give the vineyards back to a people who return to him (Hosea 2:14–15).
