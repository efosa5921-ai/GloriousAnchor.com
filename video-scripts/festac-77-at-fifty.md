On Saturday the fifteenth of January, nineteen seventy-seven, at the National Stadium in Surulere, a priest of Shango set the festival bowl alight before the Head of State and sixty thousand people, and the Second World Black and African Festival of Arts and Culture began. It ran for four weeks, to Saturday the twelfth of February, in Lagos, and in its last days in Kaduna. Seventeen thousand artists, dancers, scholars and musicians came from fifty-six countries. It was the largest gathering of Black people the world had ever seen, and the oil boom paid for all of it. This is the story of what Nigeria had before that month, what was done during it, what followed, and why the fifty years end on the twelfth of February, twenty twenty-seven.

Nigeria at independence was an agricultural power. Farming produced more than six tenths of national output and seven tenths of export earnings. The country was the world's largest exporter of palm oil and palm kernels, and the world's largest exporter of groundnuts; the sacks of the northern harvest were stacked into the pyramids of Kano that stood as tall as houses. It was the second largest cocoa producer on earth. Each region paid its own way from its own crops. External debt in nineteen sixty was about one hundred and fifty million dollars. Then came oil, and in late nineteen seventy-three the price quadrupled.

The naira was born on the first of January, nineteen seventy-three, at about sixty-six kobo to the dollar. One naira was worth a dollar and fifty cents, and by nineteen eighty it bought a dollar and eighty. In nineteen seventy-four the International Monetary Fund, trying to carry oil-importing countries through the shock, borrowed from the countries that had the money, and Nigeria was one of them. Nigeria lent the IMF one hundred and twenty million dollars, and the World Bank two hundred and forty million. For most of the years from seventy-three to eighty-two, Nigeria stood on the creditor side of the IMF's books. General Gowon said money was not Nigeria's problem, but how to spend it. On the eve of FESTAC, external debt was about three billion dollars, six per cent of national output.

The facts are not in dispute. The festival emblem was a copy of the sixteenth-century ivory mask of Queen Idia of Benin, a royal and ancestral image, carved for the occasion after the British Museum refused to lend the original. It went onto banners, programmes, coins and buses. The opening began with a twenty-one gun salute and a thousand pigeons; then a priest of Shango, the Yoruba god of thunder, lit the festival flame, with General Obasanjo presiding. The colloquium, which the organisers called the heart of the festival, opened with the Grand Patron pouring a libation of water and alcohol to the gods and the ancestors, with the Oba of Lagos at the high table.

A Christian does not have to decide whether every dancer was a devotee or every carving an idol. The public acts are enough. Deuteronomy eighteen forbids passing through the fire and consulting the dead. Paul says the things the nations sacrifice, they sacrifice to devils, and not to God. And Scripture does not grade images by the quality of the ivory. Culture is not the issue. Bezaleel was filled with the Spirit to carve for the tabernacle, and the gospel has made every African drum fit for the praise of Christ. The question is whom the object serves. When a head of state performs these rites on behalf of a nation, the nation has, in the Bible's terms, been presented to those spirits.

The regatta on the Lagos lagoon brought the war canoes and masquerades of the riverine peoples. The Grand Durbar at Kaduna put the Sultan of Sokoto and the emirs on the same field as three and a half thousand horsemen, camel riders from Katsina, and Bori dancers from Zaria. Bori is the Hausa cult of spirit possession. Nobody hid any of this. It was the point. FESTAC was conceived, from its first edition in Dakar in nineteen sixty-six, as a return to Africa's own spiritual sources after colonialism, and religion was written into the programme beside music, dance and literature.

The year after FESTAC, Nigeria went to the Eurodollar market for the first time. The one-billion-dollar jumbo loan was signed in January nineteen seventy-eight. The IMF's own historians date the change in Nigeria's borrowing from that year. By nineteen eighty-two the debt was eighteen point six billion dollars. In eighty-three the soldiers came back. In eighty-six the naira floated: two to the dollar, then four, then twenty-two, then over eighty on the street. Ninety-two at the return to democracy. Three hundred and five in twenty sixteen. Past fifteen hundred after the float of twenty twenty-three. On the thirtieth of June, twenty twenty-six, the official rate was one thousand three hundred and seventy-nine naira to the dollar, and the public debt stood at one hundred and sixty-seven trillion naira. A country that lent the World Bank money in nineteen seventy-four now owes it more than twenty billion dollars.

The economists have explained all of this: oil dependence, the collapse of farming, a currency held artificially strong, military governments that never had to tax their people, loans taken in boom years and due in bust years, corruption at every stage. All of that is true. It is also exactly what the prophets said happens. Hosea watched a nation enjoy a boom and misread it. Israel's oil, in the prophet's own word, came from God, and Israel spent it on the gods of the land. The judgment was not a thunderbolt. It was economic: "I will take away my corn in the time thereof." In nineteen seventy-seven, at the height of the wealth God had given it, Nigeria dedicated that wealth, through its head of state, to spirits that are not God. The year after, the borrowing began. Four years after, the fall.

Nigeria's Pentecostal churches, then a small and despised minority, protested the festival before it opened as a plot to revive idol worship. They were ignored. Then came the revival of the nineteen eighties. The objection has never gone away. Preachers from Chris Okafor to Felix Adunpe and David Adepoju have called FESTAC the nation's root sin and called for national repentance. The men who ran it reject the charge. At the fortieth anniversary, Obasanjo said FESTAC was staged to show the world the culture of Black peoples, and that it is not a fetish celebration or a fetish festival. We should be honest in both directions. Nigeria's troubles did not begin in nineteen seventy-seven. But what a nation's leaders do at its public altars matters to God, and sin that has never been confessed stands until it is.

We believe that at FESTAC Nigeria was leased to darkness. Not sold, because the earth is the Lord's, and no head of state can sign away what belongs to God. But leased, the way land was leased in Israel: a buyer held it and took its harvests until the fiftieth year, and then it returned to its owner by law, without a kobo paid. The lease was taken over four weeks, from the fifteenth of January to the twelfth of February, nineteen seventy-seven. So it expires on the twelfth of February, twenty twenty-seven. Until then, the powers that took it hold it with a legitimacy that Scripture itself recognises. From the thirteenth of February, the ground is gone. Any hold on Nigeria after that date is a squatter's hold.
---
kicker: Two elections, inside the last month
caption: 16 January and 6 February 2027

Here is the fact that made us write this. Under the Electoral Act of twenty twenty-two, the presidential election was fixed for the twentieth of February, twenty twenty-seven, eight days after the lease expires. The new Electoral Act of twenty twenty-six moved it to the sixteenth of January, the day after the fiftieth anniversary of the fire, and moved the governorship elections to the sixth of February, six days before the lease ends. The official reasons are the new Act's timelines and the month of Ramadan, and we report them. We believe the real reason is spiritual. The powers know the terms of their own lease to the day, and have moved Nigeria's choice of its next rulers into the last weeks in which they can lawfully influence it. But the new government takes office on the twenty-ninth of May, on ground that has already returned to its owner.
---
kicker: Luke, chapter four
caption: "To preach the acceptable year of the Lord."

Nigeria is being offered two jubilees in twenty twenty-seven. The first is the one the culture agencies are planning: a hundred countries, a second festival on the site of the first, and not a word about the fire, the libation or the mask. A festival that repeats the rites in the same weeks is not a commemoration. It is a renewal of the lease. The second is the jubilee God wrote into the law and Jesus announced in Nazareth: liberty proclaimed, debts cancelled, the land returned, and all of it on the far side of confession. One extends the lease. The other ends it. Which one takes hold of the next fifty years depends on which one the people of God treat as the real event.

So, between the fifteenth of January and the twelfth of February, twenty twenty-seven: name what was done, without apology and without contempt for Africa. Confess it, as Daniel confessed Israel's sin as his own. Pray Hosea's prayer, not Siloam's: not "whose fault was it", but "give us the vineyards again." Turn from the same idols in the church. Vote as a jubilee people. And work, because the groundnut pyramids were built by hand. The promise of Second Chronicles seven fourteen is not that the naira will recover.
