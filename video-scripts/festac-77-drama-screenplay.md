# FESTAC 77: The Lease

**A dramatised film with narrator, in thirteen scenes. Running time about 14 minutes.**

Based on the article "FESTAC 77: the four weeks Nigeria was leased to darkness, the lease that expires on 12 February 2027, and the two jubilees the nation must choose between" by Efosa Adun, gloriousanchor.com.

Every public fact in the narration (dates, figures, what was done at the festival, the moved election dates) is taken from the article and its sources. The Oyelaran family, Pastor Nwosu and Mr Hassan are invented, as is everything they say. No real person is given invented words: General Obasanjo and the other men who ran the festival appear only in the narrator's voice and in archive footage.

## Locations and dressing

- A 1970s Lagos sitting room (one set, redressed for 1986 and 2027: the same house, older each time).
- A government office with files, a rotary telephone, a large FESTAC emblem poster (the Queen Idia mask).
- A primary-school classroom with benches (the fellowship).
- A church interior (2027), large enough for a trumpet to sound.
- Exterior: the front of the National Theatre, Iganmu, at dawn (scenes 1 and 13). Permission needed.
- Archive footage, licensed or public domain, for the stadium opening, the regatta and the Durbar. If none can be licensed, these moments are played on the faces of the people watching, with sound only.
- Props: a 1977 festival programme; a 1970s one-naira note; a 1986 newspaper with a "SAP" headline; a shofar or trumpet; a wall calendar for January and February 2027 with the 16th, the 6th and the 12th circled.

## SCENE 1. COLD OPEN. THE NATIONAL THEATRE, IGANMU. DAWN, TODAY.

*Grey light. The theatre's great hat-shaped roof against the sky. A man of sixty, BABA TUNDE, stands alone on the empty forecourt in a good agbada, holding an old festival programme, its cover faded to the colour of tea. He looks at it, then at the building.*

**NARRATOR:** This building was finished in 1976 for a festival that lasted four weeks. The festival ended on the twelfth of February, 1977. On the twelfth of February, 2027, it will be fifty years to the day.

*Baba Tunde opens the programme. Inside, in a child's handwriting: "Tunde Oyelaran, age 10. Daddy took me."*

**NARRATOR:** This man was there. He was ten years old. He has carried this programme for fifty years, and he knows what that date means, because of something his mother said at a dinner table in 1976, and something a pastor said to him last year. To understand why he is standing here, you have to go back to a time when Nigeria lent money to the world.

*Title card over the theatre: **FESTAC 77: THE LEASE**.*

---

## SCENE 2. THE OYELARAN SITTING ROOM, LAGOS. EVENING, 1974.

*A new radiogram, a ceiling fan, framed photographs. CHIEF OYELARAN, in shirt and tie, home from work, opens a bottle of Star. MAMA EBUN brings in food. TUNDE, seven, is on the floor with a toy car. A television in the corner, black and white.*

**TV ANNOUNCER (V.O.):** ...and in Washington today the Federal Government concluded an agreement to lend one hundred and twenty million dollars to the International Monetary Fund's oil facility. Nigeria is one of the few countries in the world now in a position to lend to the Fund. Earlier this year the Federal Government also lent two hundred and forty million dollars to the World Bank...

**CHIEF OYELARAN:** *(raising his bottle at the television)* You hear that? Nigeria is lending to the World Bank. We are lending to the people who lend to everybody.

**MAMA EBUN:** Then let them lend some of it to the hospital at Yaba. I waited four hours today.

**CHIEF OYELARAN:** Ebun, patience. The money is coming. The Head of State said it himself: money is not our problem, the problem is how to spend it.

*He takes a one-naira note from his pocket and holds it up to Tunde.*

**CHIEF OYELARAN:** Tunde. You see this? One naira. Do you know what one naira buys in London?

**TUNDE:** No, sir.

**CHIEF OYELARAN:** One dollar and fifty cents. More than their own money. When you go to England to read law, you will be a rich man on your father's salary.

**NARRATOR:** It was true. The naira was born in January 1973 at sixty-six kobo to the dollar, and it held. Nigeria was the world's largest exporter of palm oil and of groundnuts; in Kano the sacks were stacked into pyramids as tall as houses. Then the price of oil quadrupled, and the money came in faster than anyone could spend it. External debt was three billion dollars, about six per cent of the national output, and Nigeria owed nobody anything it could not pay.

*Mama Ebun, serving, glances at the money in her husband's hand and says nothing. She touches Tunde's head.*

**MAMA EBUN:** *(quietly)* Eat your food.

## SCENE 3. THE FESTAC SECRETARIAT, LAGOS. DAY, 1976.

*A busy government office. Files, telephones, a large poster of the ivory mask of Queen Idia with the words SECOND WORLD BLACK AND AFRICAN FESTIVAL OF ARTS AND CULTURE. CHIEF OYELARAN at a desk, MR HASSAN at the next one, both in agbada.*

**MR HASSAN:** The opening ceremony programme has come back from the committee. Twenty-one gun salute. One thousand pigeons. Then the flame.

**CHIEF OYELARAN:** Who lights the flame?

**MR HASSAN:** A priest of Shango.

*A beat. Chief Oyelaran looks up.*

**CHIEF OYELARAN:** A Shango priest. In the National Stadium. In front of the Head of State and fifty nations.

**MR HASSAN:** It is our culture, Bamidele. Thunder. Fire. The gods of our fathers. The whole point of this festival is to show the world that we did not begin when the white man arrived. Senghor started it in Dakar, and now Lagos will finish it on a scale nobody has seen.

**CHIEF OYELARAN:** And the colloquium?

**MR HASSAN:** The Grand Patron will open it with a libation. Water and schnapps, to the gods and the ancestors. The Oba of Lagos will be at the high table. The Vatican is sending observers. Everybody is happy.

**CHIEF OYELARAN:** *(looking at the mask on the wall)* And that?

**MR HASSAN:** The emblem. Queen Idia of Benin. The British Museum would not lend us the original, so Emokpae has carved us a new one. It will be on the coins, the buses, the banners. For one month, that face is Nigeria.

*Chief Oyelaran nods slowly, and goes back to his file.*

**NARRATOR:** Nobody hid any of it. It was the point. The festival was conceived as a return to Africa's own spiritual sources, and religion was written into the programme beside music, dance and literature. The cost is usually put at around four hundred million dollars: a new National Theatre, a new town for the guests, a new nation's face. Nigeria could afford it. That was what everyone said.

## SCENE 4. A PRIMARY-SCHOOL CLASSROOM. NIGHT, LATE 1976.

*A dozen people on children's benches under one bulb. A hand-lettered banner: CHRIST APOSTOLIC FELLOWSHIP, YABA. PASTOR EMEKA NWOSU, young, thin, in a short-sleeved shirt, with a Bible. MAMA EBUN among the women. The singing dies down.*

**PASTOR NWOSU:** You have all seen the posters. You have all seen the mask. In January the government of this country is going to light a fire to Shango in the National Stadium and pour drink to the gods in the name of every one of us. In our name. In the name of Nigeria.

**A MAN:** Pastor, it is culture. They say it is culture.

**PASTOR NWOSU:** Pharaoh's magicians were culture. The high places of Israel were culture. Brother, listen to the word of the Lord: "Thou shalt have no other gods before me." Not "no other gods, except at festivals." We are a small people. They will not listen to us. But we will write, and we will sign, and we will pray, so that when this thing is done it will be on the record that the church of God in this land said no.

*He holds up a letter.*

**PASTOR NWOSU:** This is going to the newspapers and to the secretariat. It says what we believe: that this festival is a plot of the devil to revive the worship of idols in Nigeria. Who will sign?

*Hands go up. Mama Ebun hesitates, then raises hers.*

**NARRATOR:** The Pentecostal churches in 1977 were a small and despised minority. Scholars of the period record that they organised to protest the festival "on the grounds that it was a plot from the devil to revitalize idol worship". They were ignored.

## SCENE 5. THE OYELARAN SITTING ROOM. THE SAME NIGHT, LATER.

*Chief Oyelaran at the table with a plate. Mama Ebun comes in, puts down her Bible, sits. Tunde, in pyjamas, listens from the passage.*

**CHIEF OYELARAN:** Hassan showed me a letter today. From a fellowship in Yaba. "A plot of the devil." Signed by forty people.

*Silence.*

**CHIEF OYELARAN:** Your name is on it, Ebun.

**MAMA EBUN:** Yes.

**CHIEF OYELARAN:** I work in that secretariat. Do you know how that looks?

**MAMA EBUN:** I know how it looks to God.

**CHIEF OYELARAN:** It is a cultural festival! It is dancing and carving and poetry! The Vatican is sending people!

**MAMA EBUN:** Bamidele, who lights the fire?

*He does not answer.*

**MAMA EBUN:** Who pours the drink, and to whom? You have told me yourself. You can call it culture. The one they are pouring it to does not call it culture. He calls it an agreement.

**CHIEF OYELARAN:** *(standing, tired, not angry)* I am taking Tunde to the opening. He will remember it all his life.

**MAMA EBUN:** *(after a moment)* Yes. He will.

*She looks at the passage. Tunde ducks back into the dark.*

## SCENE 6. THE NATIONAL STADIUM, SURULERE. SATURDAY 15 JANUARY 1977.

*Archive footage if licensed; otherwise the stands, played on faces. CHIEF OYELARAN and TUNDE in the crowd, the boy with the programme. The roar. A twenty-one gun salute. A thousand pigeons rise.*

**STADIUM ANNOUNCER (V.O.):** ...the lighting of the festival flame!

*A priest of Shango, in red, crosses the field with a torch. Tunde stands on his seat. Chief Oyelaran's face: pride, and underneath it something else. The flame goes up. The crowd roars.*

**TUNDE:** Daddy! Daddy, look!

**CHIEF OYELARAN:** *(quietly)* I see it.

*Cut to: the classroom. Mama Ebun and the fellowship on their knees. Pastor Nwosu, standing, eyes open, hearing the distant roar through the window.*

**PASTOR NWOSU:** Lord, it is done in our name. Write down that it was not done in our hearts.

**NARRATOR:** On the fifteenth of January, 1977, with the military head of state presiding, a priest of the Yoruba god of thunder lit the flame that opened the festival. Scripture is plain about what such an act is: "Thou shalt not bow down thyself to them, nor serve them: for I the LORD thy God am a jealous God, visiting the iniquity of the fathers upon the children unto the third and fourth generation of them that hate me."

## SCENE 7. THE COLLOQUIUM HALL, AND THE DURBAR. JANUARY TO 12 FEBRUARY 1977.

*The colloquium: a high table, dignitaries, the Oba of Lagos among them. The Grand Patron (seen from behind, or in archive) pours from a bottle onto the ground. Chief Oyelaran stands at the back of the hall with Mr Hassan.*

**MR HASSAN:** *(whispering, pleased)* To the gods and the ancestors. Beautiful.

*Chief Oyelaran watches the liquid soak into the floor.*

**NARRATOR:** The colloquium, which the organisers called the heart of the festival, opened with a libation to the gods and the ancestors. Its working groups included one on Black civilisation and religion. On the lagoon the war canoes and the masquerades came out for the regatta.

*Archive or sound: drums, canoes, masquerades. Then the Durbar at Kaduna: three and a half thousand horsemen, camel riders, Bori dancers in trance.*

**NARRATOR:** And on Saturday the twelfth of February, at Kaduna, the Grand Durbar closed it: the Sultan and the emirs, the horsemen, and the Bori dancers of Zaria, whose cult is the cult of spirit possession. Four weeks, from the fire at Surulere to the horses at Kaduna. Four weeks of rites, libations, masquerades, and a mask held up before the nations, by a nation's leaders, in a nation's name.

*The classroom. Pastor Nwosu alone, late, the lamp low. He writes in the margin of his Bible at Leviticus 25. We see the words: "15 Jan – 12 Feb 1977. Fifty years. Lord, remember."*

**NARRATOR:** Satan is a legalist. He stood at Joshua the high priest's right hand with a case to make. He asked for Job, and was given him. Paul warned the church not to "give place to the devil", and the word for place means a foothold, an opening he is entitled to use. A nation's leaders gave him place in 1977, publicly, in the nation's name. And in the law of Moses, what is given away under pressure is not given for ever. The land cannot be sold for ever, because the land is the Lord's. It can only be leased. And every lease has a term.

## SCENE 8. THE TURN. 1978 TO 1986.

*A montage, carried by the narrator and three short played moments.*

**NARRATOR:** The year after the festival, Nigeria went to the international money markets for the first time. A one-billion-dollar "jumbo loan" in January 1978, another of seven hundred and fifty million before the year was out. The IMF's own historians date the change in Nigeria's borrowing from that year.

*1. The secretariat, 1981, now a different ministry. Chief Oyelaran, greyer, and Mr Hassan reading a newspaper: OIL REVENUE HALVED. AUSTERITY.*

**MR HASSAN:** Eighteen billion dollars. Four years ago we were lending it.

**CHIEF OYELARAN:** *(not looking up)* Four years ago.

**NARRATOR:** Oil prices rose, and the Second Republic spent as if they would rise for ever. Then they fell. By 1982 the external debt was eighteen and a half billion dollars. In 1983 the soldiers came back. By 1985 the country could not pay.

*2. The sitting room, 1986. The same furniture, worn. TUNDE, nineteen, in a university shirt, holds a letter. Mama Ebun beside him. Chief Oyelaran, retired, in a chair.*

**TUNDE:** The scholarship for England is cancelled. All of them. They say the foreign exchange is finished.

**CHIEF OYELARAN:** *(reaching for his wallet, taking out an old one-naira note, looking at it)* One dollar and fifty cents.

**MAMA EBUN:** Bamidele.

**CHIEF OYELARAN:** It was worth one dollar and fifty cents. I told him. I promised him.

**NARRATOR:** In 1986 the government took the structural adjustment programme and let the naira float. Under one to the dollar in May; two by the end of the year; four by 1988; twenty-two by 1993. Output per Nigerian, in dollars, fell from about nine hundred in 1980 to about two hundred and sixty in 1986. A generation grew up poorer than its parents in a country pumping two million barrels of oil a day.

*3. The sitting room, night. Chief Oyelaran alone, the festival programme in his hands. Mama Ebun comes to the door.*

**CHIEF OYELARAN:** *(not turning)* Ebun. The thing you said. "He calls it an agreement."

**MAMA EBUN:** Yes.

**CHIEF OYELARAN:** What was the term?

*She comes and sits beside him. She does not answer. She takes his hand.*

## SCENE 9. A LARGE CHURCH, LAGOS. 2005.

*A great hall, thousands of seats, empty on a weekday. PASTOR NWOSU, now old and heavy, walks the aisle with TUNDE, now thirty-eight, a secondary-school teacher in a tie.*

**TUNDE:** My mother is gone, Pastor. In her Bible she left your name and the date of the fellowship letter. She said to tell you she never regretted signing it.

**PASTOR NWOSU:** *(stopping)* Forty of us. In a classroom. They laughed at us, Tunde. The newspapers called us fanatics. Now look. *(he gestures at the hall)* The revival came in the eighties and the fellowships of 1977 became the biggest congregations on the face of the earth. God answered the small people. But he has not yet answered the nation.

**TUNDE:** The debt is being cancelled. Okonjo-Iweala signed in Paris. Eighteen billion written off.

**PASTOR NWOSU:** For twelve billion in cash. Good. I thank God for it. And watch: within fifteen years they will borrow it all back, because the thing that was done has not been undone. You cannot pay off a lease with the landlord's own money.

**TUNDE:** You keep saying lease.

**PASTOR NWOSU:** *(opening his old Bible to Leviticus 25, the margin note faded)* Read it. "The land shall not be sold for ever, for the land is mine." A man in hard times could give his inheritance away, and the buyer took its harvests, but only until the fiftieth year. Then the trumpet sounded, and every man returned to his possession. Without a kobo paid. Because the owner's claim never lapsed. *(tapping the margin)* Fifteenth of January to twelfth of February, 1977. I wrote it that night. Fifty years, Tunde. Fifty years.

**NARRATOR:** In 2006 the books were cleared. By 2023 Nigeria owed forty-three billion dollars abroad; by June 2026, fifty-four and a half billion. The country that lent the World Bank two hundred and forty million dollars in 1974 now owes it more than twenty billion.

## SCENE 10. THE OYELARAN SITTING ROOM. FEBRUARY 2026.

*The same house, now Baba Tunde's, a flat-screen television where the old set stood. BABA TUNDE, sixty, watching the news with his grandchildren around him.*

**TV ANNOUNCER (V.O.):** ...the Independent National Electoral Commission has announced new dates for the 2027 general elections following the signing of the Electoral Act 2026. The presidential and National Assembly elections, previously fixed for the twentieth of February, will now hold on Saturday the sixteenth of January, 2027. Governorship and state assembly elections move from the sixth of March to Saturday the sixth of February. In other news, the Centre for Black and African Arts and Civilisation says more than one hundred countries are expected for the golden jubilee of FESTAC 77 next year, and Delta State has announced FESTAC Africa 2027 for Asaba...

*Baba Tunde reaches for the remote and freezes the screen on the dates. He gets up, goes to a shelf, takes down his mother's Bible, opens it at Leviticus 25, where his father's old programme is kept as a bookmark, and reads the margin note in Pastor Nwosu's hand, copied there by his mother.*

**BABA TUNDE:** *(reading)* "Fifteenth of January to twelfth of February, 1977. Fifty years."

*He looks at the frozen television: 16 JANUARY 2027. 6 FEBRUARY 2027.*

**BABA TUNDE:** Both of them. Both of them inside.

*He picks up his phone.*

## SCENE 11. PASTOR GRACE'S OFFICE. THE NEXT DAY.

*A modern church office. PASTOR GRACE NWOSU-ADEYEMI, fifties, her father's photograph on the wall. Baba Tunde across the desk, the Bible open between them, the calendar on the wall with the three dates circled.*

**PASTOR GRACE:** My father never stopped talking about those dates. I thought it was an old man's arithmetic.

**BABA TUNDE:** The election was fixed for the twentieth of February. Eight days after the lease expires. They have moved it to the sixteenth of January: the day after the fiftieth anniversary of the fire. And the governors to the sixth of February, six days before the lease ends. Pastor, both elections are now inside the last month of the lease.

**PASTOR GRACE:** They gave reasons. Ramadan. The lawyers' timetable.

**BABA TUNDE:** Men always have reasons. "We wrestle not against flesh and blood." The men who moved the dates may have had nothing in their minds but the calendar. But the powers know the terms of their own lease to the day. An election on the twentieth of February would have been held on ground they no longer had any right to stand on. On the sixteenth of January their standing is still lawful.

*Pastor Grace stands, goes to the calendar, puts her finger on 12 February.*

**PASTOR GRACE:** And on the thirteenth?

**BABA TUNDE:** On the thirteenth the land returns to its owner. Whoever wins in January is not sworn in until the twenty-ninth of May. They will be chosen under the old lease. They will govern in a Nigeria that is no longer leased. And the church will have every right in heaven to pray them into the hand of God, or out of office.

**PASTOR GRACE:** *(slowly)* Think of a tide.

**BABA TUNDE:** Yes.

**PASTOR GRACE:** For fifty years evil in this country has swum with the tide. The looter prospered. The killer was never found. We asked Jeremiah's question: why does the way of the wicked prosper? And the answer was not that God had left Nigeria. The answer was that the lease was valid. *(she turns)* After the twelfth of February the tide turns. Evil will still be done, because men are still sinners. But it will be swimming against the current. And it will tire.

**BABA TUNDE:** "He that covereth his sins shall not prosper."

**PASTOR GRACE:** But Baba, there is a second festival. A much bigger one, for 2027, to mark the jubilee. A hundred countries. The same emblem. The same drums.

**BABA TUNDE:** *(nodding)* That is why I came. A commemoration is one thing. A bigger FESTAC for the jubilee year, by the same kind of men, with the same silence about whose altar it is: in the terms of the lease, that is not a commemoration. That is a renewal.

## SCENE 12. TWO JUBILEES. THE ROAD TO 12 FEBRUARY 2027.

*Intercut, faster. On one side: a stadium, drums, dancers, the mask on banners, fireworks, a presenter talking about tourism and the creative economy. On the other: Pastor Grace's church, full, lights low, the congregation on its knees. Baba Tunde among them. Other churches: a village chapel, a Catholic cathedral, a campus fellowship, a prayer group on a bus.*

**NARRATOR:** In 2027 Nigeria is being offered two jubilees, and they do not point in the same direction. The nation's jubilee celebrates the thing that was done. God's jubilee undoes it. The nation's jubilee is paid for, as the first was, with borrowed money. God's jubilee cancels debt. The nation's jubilee gathers the nations to look at Nigeria. God's jubilee gathers Nigerians to look at God. One needs a budget, a committee and a hundred ambassadors. The other needs a trumpet and a repentant people.

**PASTOR GRACE:** *(to the congregation)* We are not here to grumble about a festival. We are here to confess. We confess that in 1977 this nation, in all our names, lit a fire to a god that is no god, and poured drink to the dead, and we were silent, or we were few. Lord, we were few. Forgive this land.

*The congregation, aloud, a wave of sound. Baba Tunde, eyes shut, his father's programme in his hand.*

**NARRATOR:** In Leviticus the jubilee was proclaimed not with a festival but with a trumpet, on the Day of Atonement, the day the nation confessed its sin. Freedom, restoration, rest and reset, and all of them on the far side of confession. And when Jesus stood up in Nazareth and read the jubilee from Isaiah, he said: "This day is this scripture fulfilled in your ears."

**NARRATOR:** By reason of God's jubilee, according to Leviticus 25, verses 9 to 15, the powers that took the lease will no longer have the grounds to stand. The trumpet of jubilee proclaims four things: the release of slaves and captives, the cancellation of debt, the return of property, and rest for the land.

*Church after church: a trumpet is raised. The drums of the festival and the trumpets of the churches on the soundtrack together, the trumpets growing louder.*

---

## SCENE 13. THE NATIONAL THEATRE. DAWN, 12 FEBRUARY 2027, AS IT COULD BE.

**NARRATOR:** Picture the morning of the twelfth of February, 2027. What was leased in four weeks of 1977 returns to its owner that day, by a law older than Nigeria. Whether the people of God are standing here that morning, in confession and with a trumpet, or whether the nation spends the jubilee year renewing the lease at another festival, is being decided now.

*As in the cold open. Baba Tunde on the forecourt. Behind him, out of the half-light, people arrive: Pastor Grace, the congregation, others, quietly, until there are hundreds. Nobody dances. Nobody carries a banner. A young man carries a trumpet.*

**BABA TUNDE:** *(to Pastor Grace, looking at the programme)* My father took me to the opening. He wanted me to remember it all my life. *(a beat)* I have.

*He closes the programme and gives it to her.*

**BABA TUNDE:** On the twelfth of February the lease is over. We will be here.

*Pastor Grace reads aloud, from his mother's Bible.*

**PASTOR GRACE:** "And ye shall hallow the fiftieth year, and proclaim liberty throughout all the land unto all the inhabitants thereof: it shall be a jubilee unto you; and ye shall return every man unto his possession."

*She nods to the young man. He raises the trumpet. One long note over the forecourt, over the roof, over the lagoon. The sun comes up behind the theatre.*

**NARRATOR:** This is not a prophecy about an election. It is an understanding of the hour. Whether the nation signs a new lease in its jubilee year, or sounds a trumpet, is in the hands of the people of God, between now and that morning. "The Spirit of the Lord is upon me, because he hath anointed me to preach the gospel to the poor... to preach the acceptable year of the Lord."

*Hold on the trumpeter against the sunrise. Fade.*

## Production notes

**Running time.** About fourteen minutes at a natural pace when played in full; the AI-acted version cuts the dialogue to one or two lines a shot and runs seven minutes.

**The present day.** The film is meant to be watched from now until well after 12 February 2027. Scenes 1 and 13 are set at the theatre at dawn, but the narrator never says that day has come: scene 1 looks forward to it and scene 13 pictures it. Keep it that way in any re-edit. Scenes 8 and 12 are montages and can be cut to length.

**Narration.** The narrator's lines are complete and can be recorded first, so that filming has a track to cut to. Glorious Anchor's existing video pipeline can produce the narration track, the title cards, the end card and burned-in subtitles, and can assemble the filmed scenes with the narration once the footage is delivered.

**Archive.** The opening, the regatta and the Durbar exist on film (NTA, Reuters and British Pathé hold material). None of it is free to use; it must be licensed. The script is written so that each of those moments can instead be played on the faces of the people watching, with sound only, which also costs nothing and is often stronger.

**Real people.** The Head of State, the Grand Patron, the Oba of Lagos, the Sultan and the emirs are named by the narrator and seen only in archive or from behind. No actor speaks as any of them. Keep it that way.

**Casting Tunde.** Three actors: a boy of about ten, a young man of about nineteen, and a man of about sixty. Give them one physical habit in common (the way he holds the programme, with both hands).

**Music.** Talking drums under the festival scenes; a single trumpet or shofar for the jubilee; silence under the dinner-table scenes.

**Period.** 1974 to 1986: agbada and shirt-and-tie, a Peugeot 504 if one can be found, the old one-naira note, a radiogram, kerosene lamps in the classroom. The same sitting-room set, aged a little for each decade, carries the story's passage of time better than any caption.
